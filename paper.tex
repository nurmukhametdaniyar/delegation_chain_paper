\begin{abstract}
Autonomous agents built on large language models increasingly invoke tools
across organizational boundaries, frequently via the Model Context Protocol
(MCP). The receiving organization has no verifiable guarantee that such an
invocation was authorized, that its specific parameter values were
sanctioned rather than just its action type, or that the delegation chain
behind it is intact. We present DelegationChain, a protocol for
parameter-bound delegation chains between mutually distrusting
organizations. Each hop is signed over a recursive digest of the chain, and
the invocation binds exact parameter values. A typed policy language, with
closed-world parameter declarations and mandatory audience clauses, admits a
polynomial-time containment check that is sound with respect to its semantic
subset relation. A federated registry provides proof-of-possession
registration, policy ceilings pinned by the receiving organization, and
revocation by identity--key binding. We implemented the protocol in Rust and
evaluated it with a threat-mapped security suite, a differential test of the
containment procedure (no soundness violations in 108,370 well-formed scope
pairs), and a benchmark whose analysis plan was frozen before measurement.
The benchmark also tests the design choice the protocol was first built
around: aggregating BLS signatures across the chain. Aggregation is not a
net benefit in any configuration we measured. With prefix caching, a BLS
verifier is 12.2--24.2$\times$ slower than an Ed25519 one, and when every
call carries a new chain 4.9--16.0$\times$ slower, while aggregation saves
at most 8.8\% of a chain's bytes, because the token bodies, with their keys
and policies, dominate it. We therefore specify DelegationChain over per-hop
Ed25519 signatures, retaining aggregation as a measured variant; on our
hardware this instantiation verifies a three-agent chain under the paper's
example policy in 109~\textmu{}s, or 26~\textmu{}s when the delegation
prefix is cached.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Software agents built on large language models are increasingly deployed in
configurations where they invoke external tools and delegate sub-tasks to
other agents. The Model Context Protocol \citep{anthropic-mcp}, introduced in
late 2024, has emerged as a widely-adopted standard for this interaction
pattern. As deployment scales, an architectural pattern is becoming
common: agents operated by one organization invoke tools or delegate
work to agents operated by another.

Cross-organizational agent invocation creates an authorization problem that
existing infrastructure does not adequately address. Consider an agent at
Organization A, acting on behalf of a human principal, that needs to invoke
a payment-processing tool at Organization B. Organization B's tool must
decide whether to honor the request. To do so safely, B needs verifiable
answers to four questions: \emph{(i)} did the request actually originate
from an authorized agent at A, rather than from an attacker impersonating
that agent; \emph{(ii)} was the agent operating within the scope its
human principals authorized, rather than acting beyond its delegated
authority; \emph{(iii)} were the specific parameter values supplied --- the
amount, the recipient, the file path --- sanctioned by the authorization
chain, or has an intermediate party substituted them; and \emph{(iv)} has
the authorization chain itself been preserved intact, or has some step in
the chain been forged, dropped, or reordered.

Existing protocols address subsets of these questions but do not jointly
satisfy them. OAuth 2.1 \citep{oauth21} proves an agent's identity to a
resource server but does not bind authorization to specific parameter
values, and where tokens are introspected rather than self-contained it
requires a synchronous call to the authorization server on the
verification path. Its answer to crossing organizational boundaries, the
identity-chaining draft \citep{oauth-identity-chaining}, narrows scope
at each boundary but costs a round trip to the authorization servers on
both sides of every one. Macaroons \citep{birgisson2014macaroons}
provide attenuated capability delegation with policy caveats and reach
across trust domains through third-party caveats, at the cost of a
round trip to each third party to collect a discharge. SPIFFE \citep{spiffe} provides
workload identity within a trust domain but does not include parameter binding
or delegation chains. UCAN \citep{ucan-spec} extends capability delegation to
public-key settings and, in version 1.0, specifies a policy language over
command arguments, but it defines no containment relation between
capabilities and does not address MCP integration.
Among MCP-specific work, AgentBound \citep{buhler2026agentbound}
provides single-organization runtime enforcement, measurement work
\citep{zhou2026mcpauth} establishes the size of the gap, and
\citet{maloyan2026breaking} propose a protocol extension that
authenticates servers and their messages to clients; none provides a
cross-organizational proof that an agent's invocation was authorized.
The Agent Identity Protocol \citep{prakash2026aip} is the closest design:
it offers locally verifiable delegation chains built on Biscuit, but its
tokens do not bind an invocation's parameter values, and it has no
registration or federation model and defers revocation. ACLE-MCP
\citep{ding2026aclemcp} issues a short-lived lease for each protected
call that carries parameter bounds and attests the provider-side
workload; its leases are issued per call rather than verified from a
chain built once per delegation.

This paper presents DelegationChain, an authorization protocol for
cross-organizational agent invocation. A receiving organization should be
able to verify an invocation on its own: that it is bound to the exact
parameter values of the call, that it was checked against policies both
organizations can reason about, and that the delegation chain behind it is
intact. DelegationChain provides this with three components. Delegation
chains are built on a recursive chain digest and signed hop by hop, so
that each signature commits to every earlier hop. A typed policy language,
with closed-world parameter declarations and mandatory audience clauses,
has a containment check that is polynomial and sound with respect to its
semantic subset relation. A federated registry provides proof-of-possession
registration, policy ceilings pinned by the receiving organization, and
revocation by identity--key binding.

We implemented the protocol, tested it against its threat model and
against a differential oracle for its policy language, and measured it
under an analysis plan fixed before any measurement. The measurement also
settled the design choice the protocol was first built around: aggregating
BLS signatures \citep{boneh2001bls,bgls2003aggregate} across the chain, so
that a chain of any length carries one 96-byte signature. Aggregation is
not a net benefit in any configuration we measured. With prefix caching a
BLS verifier is 12.2--24.2$\times$ slower than an Ed25519 one, and
aggregation saves at most 8.8\% of a chain's bytes. We therefore specify
DelegationChain over per-hop Ed25519 signatures \citep{rfc8032}, and keep
aggregation as a measured variant.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
This paper makes five contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{A threat model} for cross-organizational agent invocation,
          organized into six families of attack and separating what the
          protocol prevents from what it only bounds
          (\cref{sec:threat-model}).
    \item \emph{A protocol} for parameter-bound delegation chains, built
          from a recursive chain digest and per-hop signatures, with
          closed-world parameter declarations and mandatory audience
          clauses, policy ceilings pinned by the receiving organization,
          and revocation by identity--key binding. It is specified to the
          level of a reference implementation
          (\cref{sec:protocol,sec:identity}).
    \item \emph{A policy language}, typed, whose containment procedure is
          polynomial and sound with respect to its semantic subset
          relation, validated by a differential oracle
          (\cref{sec:policy,sec:eval-correctness}).
    \item \emph{A reference implementation and its security evaluation}:
          a threat-mapped security suite, concurrent-replay tests,
          cache-equivalence tests and fuzzing
          (\cref{sec:impl-implementation,sec:eval-correctness}).
    \item \emph{A performance evaluation under a frozen plan}, including a
          negative result: BLS aggregation does not pay for agent
          delegation chains. We explain why, and give the cost of the
          recommended instantiation
          (\cref{sec:eval-method,sec:eval-results,sec:eval-claims}).
\end{enumerate}
We report the aggregation result in full because the design is a natural
one: compact signatures over multi-hop chains are an obvious goal, and the
cost of reaching it is not visible until the alternatives are measured side
by side.

\paragraph{Scope and limitations.}
The protocol is implemented and evaluated, but not formally verified. The
security analysis consists of proof sketches; symbolic verification in
Tamarin or ProVerif remains future work (\cref{sec:future-work}). The
performance results come from one machine, and are reported with the
frozen plan and every deviation from it (\cref{sec:eval-method}).

\paragraph{Paper organization.}
\Cref{sec:background} reviews MCP, the two signature schemes, and
capability-based authorization. \Cref{sec:threat-model} defines the threat
model. \Cref{sec:protocol} describes the protocol: chain digest

\Cref{sec:discussion} discusses related work, limitations and future work,

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

This section reviews the cryptographic and protocol background required to
follow the design. Readers familiar with MCP, Ed25519 and BLS signatures
may skim.

\subsection{Model Context Protocol}
\label{sec:bg-mcp}

The Model Context Protocol (MCP) \citep{anthropic-mcp} is a JSON-RPC-based
protocol standardizing how AI agents discover and invoke external tools.
An MCP \emph{host} (typically an LLM-based agent) communicates with one or
more MCP \emph{servers}, each exposing a set of tools. Tools are described
by name, a typed parameter schema, and documentation. The host requests a
tool invocation by sending a JSON-RPC message specifying the tool name and
parameter values; the server executes the tool and returns the result.

MCP's design assumes that the host and server are mutually trusted, an
assumption appropriate for the single-organization deployments common in
its early adoption. As MCP deployments scale to cross-organizational
configurations, this assumption becomes untenable. A server invoked by an
unknown agent has no cryptographic basis on which to decide whether the
invocation is authorized. The protocol's current security guidance relies
on transport-layer authentication (typically OAuth) which addresses caller
identity but not the broader authorization questions enumerated in
\cref{sec:introduction}. The gap is not hypothetical: a measurement study
of real-world remote MCP servers \citep{zhou2026mcpauth} identified 7,973
live servers and found that 40.55\% expose tools without any
authentication at all, and that among the OAuth-enabled servers it was
able to probe, every one exhibited at least one authentication flaw.

That measurement predates the current revision of the specification,
2026-07-28 \citep{anthropic-mcp}. The revision deprecates Dynamic Client
Registration, the source of the most prevalent flaw class Zhou et al.\
report, in favor of Client ID Metadata Documents. It also makes the core
protocol stateless, removing the initialization handshake so that every
request carries its own protocol version and client information. Neither
change touches the question this paper addresses. The client
information a request carries is self-declared, and authorization
remains a matter of OAuth tokens scoped to a server, not of evidence
that a particular call was sanctioned.

\subsection{Signature Schemes}
\label{sec:bg-bls}

\paragraph{Ed25519.}
Ed25519 is the instance of EdDSA over the twisted Edwards form of
Curve25519, specified in RFC~8032 \citep{rfc8032}. Public keys are 32
bytes, signatures 64 bytes, and verifying a signature is one signature
check, with no pairing. Verification comes in variants that differ in
which encodings they accept. RFC~8032 requires the scalar $S$ of a
signature to be reduced ($S < L$), and with that check
\citet{brendel2021ed25519} prove Ed25519 strongly unforgeable (SUF-CMA).
Rejecting small-order points as well, in the public key and in the
signature's $R$, adds resistance to key-substitution attacks. The
default instantiation of DelegationChain signs each hop with Ed25519
and verifies with strict verification, which performs these checks
(\cref{sec:protocol-primitives}).

\paragraph{BLS and aggregation.}
The Boneh--Lynn--Shacham (BLS) scheme \citep{boneh2001bls} is the scheme
behind DelegationChain's aggregate variant (\cref{sec:aggregate-variant}).
It is built from bilinear pairings on elliptic curves, and is specified
for interoperable use by \citet{ietf-bls-signature} on the BLS12-381
curve \citep{ietf-pairing-curves}. A signature is a single point in
$\G{2}$ (96 bytes compressed) and a public key a point in $\G{1}$ (48
bytes compressed); verifying one signature is a pairing check.

Its attraction is \emph{aggregation}. Given signatures
$\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_n$ on distinct messages $m_1, \ldots, m_n$,
their sum $\sigma_{\text{agg}} = \sum_i \sigma_i$ is itself a point of
$\G{2}$, and its validity with respect to the public keys and messages
is checked by a single multi-pairing. Aggregate signatures of this kind
were introduced, and proved unforgeable for distinct messages, by
\citet{bgls2003aggregate}; the CFRG draft's basic scheme adopts the same
rule, rejecting any aggregate in which two messages coincide
\citep{ietf-bls-signature}. The precondition is that the
\emph{messages} be distinct, not the signers, which matters for
delegation chains, where the same agent may legitimately appear at more
than one hop. The recursive chain digest of \cref{sec:protocol-digest}
guarantees distinct messages at every hop by construction, since each
$m_k$ commits to $m_{k-1}$.

Aggregation replaces $n$ signatures with one 96-byte $\G{2}$ element. It
also makes verification cheaper than verifying $n$ BLS signatures
independently, because the Miller loops of a multi-pairing share part of
their work and need a single final exponentiation. It does not make it
$O(1)$: with distinct messages the cost still grows with $n$. The
better-known hazard of BLS aggregation, the rogue-key attack, arises when
signers sign a \emph{common} message. Defenses for that setting include
proof-of-possession at registration \citep{ristenpart2007pop} and the
alternative analyzed by \citet{boneh2018compact}; neither is needed here,
because distinct messages are themselves the rogue-key defense of the
basic scheme \citep{ietf-bls-signature}.

Whether aggregation's savings outweigh the cost of BLS verification is an
empirical question; \cref{sec:impl} answers it.

\subsection{Capability-Based Authorization}
\label{sec:bg-capabilities}

The protocol design draws on the capability-based security tradition
\citep{miller2006robust}. In a capability system, authority is represented
by unforgeable tokens (capabilities) that the holder can present to obtain
access. Capabilities can be \emph{attenuated}: a holder may produce a
restricted version of a capability and delegate the restricted version to
another party, without being able to grant authority exceeding what they
themselves hold.

Macaroons \citep{birgisson2014macaroons} apply this model to web
authorization. A macaroon is an HMAC chain in which each link adds
``caveats'' --- predicates restricting the macaroon's use. First-party
caveats are checked by the target service itself, and verification
recomputes the HMAC chain using a secret that the target service knows.

Macaroons do address the cross-domain case, through \emph{third-party
caveats}. A caveat may name a principal other than the target service;
to use the macaroon, the holder must obtain a \emph{discharge macaroon}
from that principal attesting that the caveat is satisfied, and present
it alongside. The discharge is cryptographically bound to the macaroon
it discharges, and the target service verifies it without sharing a
secret with the third party. It is therefore not correct to say that
Macaroons cannot cross organizational boundaries, and we do not say so.

The difference that matters for our setting is where the work happens.
A third-party caveat costs a round trip: before presenting the macaroon,
the holder must contact each named third party and collect a discharge,
and the third party must be reachable at that moment. DelegationChain
places the equivalent authority in signatures the verifier checks
directly against registry-resolved public keys, with no discharge
collection step on the request path.

UCAN \citep{ucan-spec} extends capability attenuation to public-key
settings, signing DAG-CBOR-encoded tokens in a Varsig-headed envelope
with Ed25519, P-256 or secp256k1.
UCAN is designed for Web3 contexts and lacks features specific to our
setting: a containment relation between capabilities (UCAN 1.0 does
specify a policy language over command arguments), and MCP transport
binding.

DelegationChain inherits the conceptual framework of attenuated
capabilities from this tradition. Our contribution is the combination of
public-key verification (extending Macaroons' attenuation model across
organizational boundaries), parameter binding to the specific
invocation, an explicit policy language with
termination and containment guarantees, and a federated identity
infrastructure.

\section{Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat-model}

This section defines the threat model under which DelegationChain operates.
We state the protocol's assets, the assumed adversary capabilities, the
threats we defend against, the threats we explicitly do not defend against,
and the security properties we seek to provide.

\subsection{Assets and Principals}
\label{sec:threat-assets}

The assets we seek to protect are: \emph{(i)} the integrity of tool
invocations, in the sense that an accepted invocation faithfully
represents the authorization granted by the originating principals;
\emph{(ii)} the provenance of authorization chains, in the sense that the
chain delivered to a verifier accurately records the parties that authorized
each step; and \emph{(iii)} the integrity of audit records derived from
accepted chains.

The principals participating in the protocol are: the \emph{human operator},
who initiates a request and whose authority is the ultimate source of
permissions; the \emph{orchestrator agent}, which receives the initial
authorization and may delegate to other agents; \emph{specialist agents},
which receive delegated authority and either invoke tools directly or
delegate further; the \emph{tool} or \emph{MCP server}, which receives
authorization chains and decides whether to honor invocations; the
\emph{signing service}, a hosted component that holds an agent's private
key and applies policy enforcement before signing (it receives the body
it is asked to sign, with the predecessor digest $m_{k-1}$, and computes
$m_k$ itself, since a service shown only a digest could enforce
nothing); the \emph{approval service}, which obtains human approval for
actions requiring it and signs approval receipts; the \emph{issuance
service}, which signs session tokens for its organization; and the
\emph{registry}, which issues identity certificates binding each of these
principals to a public key and a role. At minimum, a signing service
refuses to sign a body that does not name its own agent's identifier and
key as the signer.

\subsection{Adversary Capabilities}
\label{sec:threat-adversary}

We model a probabilistic polynomial-time adversary with the following
capabilities. The adversary can observe and modify all network traffic
between principals (a standard Dolev-Yao adversary on the network). The
adversary can compromise an agent's runtime environment, gaining the
ability to instruct the signing service to sign messages of the
adversary's choice within the scope permitted by policy. The adversary can
inject content into agent inputs to attempt prompt injection attacks. The
adversary cannot, by default, compromise hardware security modules, the
signing service infrastructure, the approval service infrastructure, the
registry, or human approvers' devices; threats involving these
compromises are addressed separately and explicitly.

\subsection{Threats Within Scope}
\label{sec:threat-scope}

The threats in scope fall into six families. Variants within a family
share a \emph{target} --- what the attacker is trying to subvert --- and
not necessarily a defense; \cref{sec:security-threats} maps each variant
individually to the theorem or mechanism that answers it. Threats are
labelled for cross-reference in the security analysis
(\cref{sec:security}).

We divide them by what the protocol actually does about them rather than
by family. \Cref{sec:threat-within-scope} lists what it \emph{prevents}.
\Cref{sec:threat-bounded} lists what it only \emph{bounds}: the attack
remains possible, and the protocol limits what it can achieve. Both are
in scope, in the sense that the design is answerable for them, but a
reader who took the second group for the first would credit the protocol
with guarantees it does not provide. The division cuts across two
families --- \threatref{4a} and \threatref{5b} are bounded while the
rest of T4 and T5 are prevented --- and we would rather have an untidy
grouping than a tidy overclaim.

\subsubsection{Threats the protocol prevents}
\label{sec:threat-within-scope}

\paragraph{T1 --- Forgery of tokens and chains.}

\threat{1a}{Token forgery.} An adversary attempts to produce a valid
authorization token without holding the corresponding signing key.

\threat{1b}{Chain forgery and reordering.} An adversary attempts to
construct an authorization chain that was not produced by the legitimate
delegation sequence, including reordering hops, dropping hops, or
inserting unauthorized hops.

\paragraph{T2 --- Misrepresentation of granted authority.}
The two variants share a target --- the authorization that reaches the
verifier is not the authorization that was granted --- but not a
defense: \threatref{2a} is defeated by the signature over the parameter
encoding, \threatref{2b} by the containment check.

\threat{2a}{Parameter substitution.} An adversary, positioned between a
signer and a verifier, attempts to alter parameter values in an
authorized invocation (e.g., changing the destination of a transfer)
while preserving the appearance of valid authorization.

\threat{2b}{Scope escalation.} An agent attempts to delegate more
authority than it was itself granted.

\paragraph{T3 --- Reuse of valid tokens.}

\threat{3a}{Replay across sessions.} An adversary captures a valid
authorization chain and attempts to use it after its intended validity
window has expired.

\threat{3b}{Replay within validity window.} An adversary captures a
valid authorization chain and attempts to use it more than once within
its validity window.

\threat{3c}{Cross-context token reuse.} An adversary attempts to reuse a
token issued in one context (e.g., for a delegation) as if it had been
issued in another context (e.g., for an invocation).

\threat{3d}{Cross-verifier reuse.} An adversary presents a chain that
one verifier accepted to a different verifier that accepts the same
partner policy and exposes the same tool and action names. Examples are
a production and a staging deployment, two regions with separate nonce
caches, or two organizations that both pinned the same published
policy.

\paragraph{T4 --- Subversion of the approval path.}
The remaining variant of this family, \threatref{4a}, is bounded rather
than prevented and appears in \cref{sec:threat-bounded}.

\threat{4b}{Approval routing attack.} An agent routes approval requests
to a permissive approval service when policy requires a stricter one.

\threat{4c}{Approval reuse across invocations.} An adversary attempts to
reuse a valid approval receipt with an invocation different from the one
approved.

\threat{4d}{Stale approval after parameter modification.} An adversary
attempts to modify invocation parameters after approval has been
obtained.

\paragraph{T5 --- Attacks on the identity infrastructure.}
The variant \threatref{5b} is bounded rather than prevented and appears
in \cref{sec:threat-bounded}.

\threat{5a}{Key misattribution at registration.} An adversary attempts
to have the registry certify, under the adversary's own identifier, a
public key it does not control --- typically another principal's. The
adversary gains no ability to sign. What it breaks is the binding
between keys and parties that non-repudiation depends on: the key's
real holder can disown signatures made under it.

\threat{5c}{Revocation suppression.} An adversary suppresses revocation
information from reaching verifiers, extending the window during which
compromised credentials remain accepted.

\threat{5d}{Certificate substitution.} An adversary substitutes a
different certificate for the same identity during the registration or
resolution process.

\threat{5e}{Role confusion.} An adversary holding a key certified for
one role --- an agent, a signing service or an approval service --- uses
it to sign in another. For instance, it signs a session token naming
itself as issuer, to obtain its organization's full policy ceiling
rather than the authority it was granted.

\subsubsection{Threats the protocol bounds but does not prevent}
\label{sec:threat-bounded}

The protocol limits the damage the following attacks can do, but does
not prevent them, and we do not claim that it does.
\Cref{sec:security-threats} states what the bound is in each case.

\paragraph{T4 --- Subversion of the approval path (one variant).}

\threat{4a}{Compromised approval service.} An adversary obtains the
signing key of an approval service and issues fraudulent approvals. The
protocol cannot prevent this: a valid signature under a registered
approval key is, by construction, a valid receipt. What it does is
contain the consequences. Approval receipts are verified separately from
the chain signatures (\cref{sec:protocol-approvals}), and an approver's
certificate is accepted only for receipts, never for a session or
delegation body (\cref{sec:protocol-verification}). A compromised
approval service therefore cannot forge delegation chains, cannot
enlarge any agent's scope, and cannot attest to anything beyond what it
was already trusted to attest to; the blast radius is exactly the set of
invocations that policy routes through that service. Detection and
recovery belong to the identity infrastructure --- transparency log
monitoring, revocation and rotation (\cref{sec:identity}). Policies name
approval services by identifier, and their keys are resolved and checked
for revocation at verification time, so revoking a compromised approval
key takes effect without a new policy version.

\paragraph{T5 --- Attacks on the identity infrastructure (one variant).}

\threat{5b}{Compromised registry signing key.} An adversary obtains a
registry's root signing key and issues fraudulent certificates. The
protocol cannot prevent this, and the security arguments of
\cref{sec:security} assume an honest root
(\cref{sec:security-assumptions}). The damage is bounded in two ways. A
registry root is trusted only for identifiers in its own organization's
namespace (\cref{sec:identity-resolution}), so the compromise cannot
reach another organization's identities. And sessions obtained through
certificates it mints stay within the policies verifiers have pinned for
that organization (\cref{sec:policy}). Recovery requires re-establishing
the root out of band (\cref{sec:identity-rotation}).

\paragraph{T6 --- Misuse of an agent within its own policy.}

\threat{6a}{Prompt injection within policy.} An adversary, controlling
content fed to an agent, induces the agent to take actions that are
within the agent's permitted policy but contrary to the principal's
intent.

\threat{6b}{Confused deputy in delegation.} An agent is induced to sign
a delegation token favoring an adversary by manipulation of the inputs
the agent uses to construct delegation decisions.

\subsection{Threats Outside Scope}
\label{sec:threat-outside}

The following threats are explicitly outside our scope. Defending against
them requires mechanisms orthogonal to the protocol.

A fully compromised signing service can produce signatures on any
messages within an agent's policy; the protocol cannot prevent this and
relies on operational hardening (HSM-based key custody, monitored
access). Compromise of an approval service is treated in
\cref{sec:threat-bounded} rather than here, because the protocol does
bound its effect even though it cannot prevent it. Side-channel attacks on cryptographic implementations are
out of scope; we rely on the security properties of well-audited
signature libraries such as \texttt{ed25519-dalek} and \texttt{blst}. Attacks on the human approver's device or
authentication mechanism are out of scope; the protocol assumes a trusted
path between the approval service and the human. Denial-of-service attacks
on network infrastructure are out of scope. Post-quantum security is out
of scope for this version; neither Ed25519 nor BLS12-381 is
post-quantum secure, and migration to a post-quantum scheme is left to
future work.

\subsection{Security Properties Sought}
\label{sec:threat-properties}

We seek to provide the following security properties, each of which is
formally stated and argued in \cref{sec:security}:

\begin{description}
    \item[Authenticity.] Authorization tokens are unforgeable: no PPT
    adversary lacking access to a signing key can produce a token that
    verifies under the corresponding public key, except with negligible
    probability.
    \item[Parameter binding.] A token authorizing an invocation with
    parameter values $P$ is invalid for any parameter values $P' \neq P$.
    \item[Chain integrity.] The signatures over a delegation chain cover
    the entire chain content; modification, reordering, truncation, or
    extension of the chain invalidates them.
    \item[Scope monotonicity.] In any valid chain, the session scope is
    contained in a policy the verifier has pinned for the issuing
    organization, and the scope at hop $k+1$ is contained in the scope
    at hop $k$.
    \item[Non-repudiation.] Neither party to a verified action can
    plausibly deny it, assuming honest signing key management.
    \item[Bounded revocation propagation.] Once a registry has issued a
    revocation assertion, the additional window before verifiers cease
    accepting tokens under the revoked binding is bounded by a
    configurable parameter (default: 120 seconds). Detection of the
    compromise and its reporting to the registry precede this window and
    are properties of the deploying organization's monitoring rather
    than of the protocol. The protocol bounds propagation, not total
    exposure.
\end{description}

\section{Protocol Design}
\label{sec:protocol}

This section presents the cryptographic core of DelegationChain: the
participants, the token types, the chain digest construction that binds
each hop's signature to every earlier hop, the encoding of
parameter-bound authorization, the handling of approval receipts, the
verifier algorithm, and the aggregate variant. We describe the design at
a level of detail sufficient to support the security analysis of
\cref{sec:security} and the reference implementation of
\cref{sec:impl}. Bit-level details beyond those given here --- field
numbering within each structure, and the exact byte layout of the wire
format --- are not fixed by this paper; the specification published with
the reference implementation fixes them for that implementation
(see the data and code availability statement).

\subsection{Participants and Tokens}
\label{sec:protocol-participants}

A DelegationChain authorization chain has the following participants, in
the order they contribute to the chain. The \emph{issuer}~$I$ is the
organization's issuance service, holding a certificate of kind
\texttt{issuer} from its registry, which issues an initial session token
to the orchestrator agent at the start of an authorized session. The
registry's root key certifies the issuer but never signs sessions
itself, so it can stay offline. The
\emph{orchestrator}~$A_1$ is the agent that receives the session token
and initiates the workflow. Intermediate \emph{specialist
agents}~$A_2, \ldots, A_{N-1}$ may be delegated sub-tasks. The
\emph{invoking agent}~$A_N$ is the agent that finally invokes a tool at
the verifier. The \emph{verifier}~$V$ is the receiving tool or MCP
server. When policy requires human approval, an \emph{approval service}
contributes a separately-signed receipt.

The chain contains three token types. The \emph{session token},
signed by the issuer, authorizes the orchestrator to operate within a
scope for a bounded duration. \emph{Delegation tokens}, signed by
intermediate agents $A_k$ for $1 \le k < N$, attenuate authority and
pass it to the next agent. The \emph{invocation token}, signed by the
final agent $A_N$, binds the invocation to specific tool, action, and
parameter values. The chain delivered to the verifier consists of all
token bodies and one signature per hop; under the aggregate variant
(\cref{sec:aggregate-variant}), a single aggregate signature replaces
the per-hop signatures.

\subsection{Cryptographic Primitives}
\label{sec:protocol-primitives}

DelegationChain is parameterized by a signature scheme
$\Sigma = (\mathsf{KeyGen}, \Sign, \Verify)$, assumed existentially
unforgeable under chosen-message attack. Every signed message is a
32-byte SHA-256 digest. We specify two instantiations. The default signs
each hop with Ed25519 \citep{rfc8032} and verifies with strict
verification, which rejects non-canonical encodings and small-order
points (\cref{sec:bg-bls}). The aggregate variant
(\cref{sec:aggregate-variant}) uses BLS signatures on BLS12-381 and
aggregates them across the chain. Within one deployment, registries,
approval services and chain signers use the same scheme, so certificates
and receipts are signed with $\Sigma$ too.

SHA-256 is used for all non-signature hashing, including the chain
digest construction.

\subsection{Chain Digest Construction}
\label{sec:protocol-digest}

The central cryptographic construction is a recursive digest chain
binding each hop to all prior hops. We use domain separation tags to
ensure tokens of different types cannot be confused (preventing
cross-context reuse, \threatref{3c}). Tags are 8-byte ASCII strings with a
null terminator: \texttt{TAG\_SES} for sessions, \texttt{TAG\_DEL} for
delegations, \texttt{TAG\_INV} for invocations.

Let $\Canon(\cdot)$ denote canonical encoding (deterministic CBOR per
RFC 8949 §4.2.1, with the additional rules summarized in
\cref{sec:protocol-encoding}).

\paragraph{Session message.}
The issuer signs:
\begin{equation}
m_0 = H(\text{TAG\_SES} \,\|\, \Canon(\text{SessionBody}))
\end{equation}
where SessionBody contains the issuer's identifier and public key
(\texttt{issuer\_id}, \texttt{issuer\_pk}), the orchestrator's identifier and public key
(\texttt{subject\_id}, \texttt{subject\_pk}), a session identifier
(\texttt{session\_id}), a
hash committing to the governing policy (\texttt{policy\_hash}), a
scope expression, issue and expiry timestamps, and a fresh nonce. The
issue timestamp is informational. No check in the verifier depends on
it, since, like every timestamp a signer supplies, it is not evidence of
when signing happened (\cref{sec:identity-revocation}).

\paragraph{Delegation messages.}
For each intermediate hop $A_k$ with $1 \le k < N$:
\begin{equation}
m_k = H(\text{TAG\_DEL} \,\|\, m_{k-1} \,\|\, \Canon(\text{DelegationBody}_k))
\end{equation}
where DelegationBody$_k$ contains the delegator's identifier and public
key (\texttt{delegator\_id}, \texttt{delegator\_pk}), the delegatee's
identifier and public key (\texttt{delegatee\_id},
\texttt{delegatee\_pk}), the attenuated sub-scope, its hop index $k$
(\texttt{hop\_index}), the parent session's identifier
(\texttt{session\_id}), expiry, and a fresh nonce. The hop index and
session identifier are redundant with the digest recursion, which
already fixes each delegation's position and session. The verifier
checks them anyway, in phase 1, because doing so rejects a chain spliced
from two sessions, or with a hop out of place, before any signature is
verified. The delegator's
identifier is carried explicitly so that a verifier can resolve the
signer's certificate from the body it is checking, rather than having
to reach back into the preceding token. The critical property is that
$m_k$ commits to $m_{k-1}$ via the hash, recursively committing to the
entire chain history.

\paragraph{Invocation message.}
The final hop $A_N$ signs:
\begin{equation}
m_N = H(\text{TAG\_INV} \,\|\, m_{N-1} \,\|\, \Canon(\text{InvocationBody}))
\end{equation}
where InvocationBody contains the invoker's identifier and public key
(\texttt{invoker\_id}, \texttt{invoker\_pk}), the identifier of the
verifier the invocation is addressed to (\texttt{aud}, a
\texttt{service} identifier; \cref{sec:identity-registration}), the
target tool
and action identifiers, the canonically encoded parameter values, a
hash of the parameters (defense-in-depth against encoding bugs), one
approval receipt for each approval service the policy requires (possibly
none; \cref{sec:protocol-approvals}), and timing and nonce fields.

\paragraph{Signatures.}
Each participant signs its message: $\sigma_k = \Sign(\sk_k, m_k)$, for
$k = 0, \ldots, N$, where $\sk_0$ denotes the issuer's key $\sk_I$,
since the issuer signs the session body. In the default instantiation the chain carries
$\sigma_0, \ldots, \sigma_N$, one 64-byte Ed25519 signature per hop.
Because $m_k$ commits to $m_{k-1}$, each $\sigma_k$ authenticates the
entire chain prefix up to hop $k$; a verifier that has already verified
a prefix can therefore cache that result and, for a later chain with the
same prefix, verify only the new hop's signature
(\cref{sec:eval-results}).

\paragraph{Role distinguishability by position.}
The verifier reconstructs the $m_k$ values from received token bodies
using the tag determined by token position: \texttt{TAG\_SES} for the
session token, \texttt{TAG\_DEL} for each intermediate delegation
token, and \texttt{TAG\_INV} for the invocation token. Every body also
carries an explicit \texttt{kind} field, with value \texttt{session},
\texttt{delegation}, or \texttt{invocation}, inside its signed encoding.
The verifier \emph{rejects} chains where the position-derived tag does
not match a body's \texttt{kind} --- preventing chain-extension attacks
that would attempt to reuse an invocation token as a delegation, or vice
versa. Such a body would fail signature verification in any case, since
the verifier would hash it under a tag it was not signed with. The
explicit field lets the verifier reject it in phase 1, before any
signature is verified, and removes any ambiguity about how a body is to
be parsed.

\subsection{Parameter Binding}
\label{sec:protocol-params}

Parameter binding is achieved by including the canonical encoding of
parameter values directly in the message $m_N$ that the invoking agent
signs. Any modification to parameter values --- changing an amount,
substituting a recipient, altering a file path --- changes the
canonical encoding, which changes $m_N$, which invalidates $\sigma_N$
(or, under the aggregate variant, the aggregate).

To support nested parameter structures (e.g.,
\texttt{order.payment.amount}), parameter values are encoded as a
nested CBOR map with text-string keys sorted lexicographically by
encoded form (per RFC 8949 §4.2.1). Parameter values are restricted to
unsigned and signed integers, byte strings, NFC-normalized text
strings, booleans, null, and nested maps and arrays of the same. Float
values and tagged values are disallowed in parameter encodings, to
eliminate edge cases around floating-point comparison and the
introduction of unexpected types. Parameter map \emph{keys} are
text strings and are NFC-normalized on the same terms as text values.
Without an explicit rule for keys, two implementations could
canonically re-encode the same logical parameter map to different byte
strings, and the re-encoding check of \cref{sec:protocol-encoding}
would diverge between them.

Parameter binding is a cryptographic property, not an authorization
one. It guarantees that the values a verifier reads are the values the
invoking agent signed; it says nothing about whether those values were
permitted. Whether they were is decided by the policy evaluation of
\cref{sec:policy-eval}, whose closed-world treatment of undeclared
parameters is what turns binding into constraint.

The InvocationBody also includes \texttt{params\_hash}, a SHA-256
digest of the canonical parameter encoding. The verifier recomputes
this hash from the encoded parameters and rejects any chain where it
does not match. This is redundant with the signature check but serves
as defense-in-depth against subtle encoding bugs in implementations.

\subsection{Approval Receipts}
\label{sec:protocol-approvals}

When policy requires human approval for an action, the InvocationBody
carries an \emph{approval receipt} from each approval service the
policy requires, signed by that registered service. The receipt binds the human's approval decision to the
specific invocation being approved.

The flow is as follows. The agent constructs the InvocationBody
\emph{except} for the receipts field, computes the invocation digest of
this preliminary body (defined below), and submits the digest along with the full body
to the required approval service. The service presents the proposed
invocation to a human approver. On approval, the service signs an
\emph{approval body} containing the service's identifier
and public key (\texttt{approver\_id}, \texttt{approver\_pk}), the
invocation digest, opaque
attestation data identifying the human approver, and issue and expiry
timestamps (\texttt{iat}, \texttt{exp}). The signed receipt is returned to the
agent, which embeds it in the now-complete InvocationBody and proceeds
with chain construction.

Receipts are signed under their own domain separation tag, distinct
from those of chain signatures and registration proofs. The kind check
of phase 7 already stops a key from being used across these roles, so
the separate tag is defense in depth.

The \emph{invocation digest} is
\[
\mathrm{InvocationDigest}(B_N) =
  H(\texttt{TAG\_IVD} \,\|\, \Canon(B_N \text{ without its receipts field})).
\]
The receipts field is omitted when there are no receipts, so this is
exactly the digest of the preliminary body the agent submitted, and the
tag separates it from $m_N$. Receipts are ordered by approver
identifier, bytewise ascending, with at most one per approval service.
A body that breaks either rule, or carries an empty receipts field, is
malformed. A receipt from an approval service that the policy does not
require for the invocation is ignored.

The approval signature is verified separately from the chain
signatures. This decoupling has two consequences. First, the security
argument for chain integrity does not depend on approval service trust:
compromise of an approval service does not enable chain forgery. Second,
approval verification adds, per receipt, one signature verification
(under the aggregate variant's BLS keys, a hash to $\G{2}$ and a
two-pairing check). Receipts are typically zero or one per
invocation.

Policy can require approvals from multiple services (logical AND); the
required service identifiers are part of the policy that the session
token's \texttt{policy\_hash} commits to. The policy names approval
services by identifier only, never by key. Their keys are resolved at
verification time like any other signer's --- root, namespace, kind
\texttt{approver}, expiry and revocation (\cref{sec:identity}) --- so
revoking or rotating an approval service's key takes effect without a
new policy version and without every partner re-pinning. The policy
hash is fixed at session start and does not vary with where the agent
sends its approval request, so an agent that routes to a different
(perhaps more permissive) service still presents the correct hash. What
it cannot present is a receipt from a service the policy requires. The
verifier, having resolved the policy that the hash commits to, and
having checked that every scope in the chain is contained in it
(\cref{sec:policy}), obtains an approval requirement no weaker than the
policy's own, and rejects a chain whose receipts do not cover it. This
is the defense against the approval routing attack (\threatref{4b}).

\subsection{Verification}
\label{sec:protocol-verification}

The verifier algorithm proceeds in phases, ordered to fail fast on
cheap checks before performing expensive cryptographic operations.
\Cref{alg:verify,alg:verify2} state the procedure; the paragraphs that
follow comment on individual phases. Every \textbf{reject} is terminal.
The only decision-relevant state the procedure changes is the nonce
cache, in its final step, and that step is an atomic insert-if-absent.
Resolution and policy loading may fill caches
(\cref{sec:identity-resolution}). That affects the cost of later
verifications but not, within the propagation bound of
\cref{sec:identity-revocation}, their outcome. A chain that fails any
check therefore leaves the nonce cache exactly as it found it.

Three abbreviations keep the statement short, all of them reading fields
named in \cref{sec:protocol-digest}. Write $\mathrm{sid}(B_k)$ for the
identifier of the party that signed $B_k$: \texttt{issuer\_id} for the
session body, \texttt{delegator\_id} for a delegation body,
\texttt{invoker\_id} for the invocation body. Write $\mathrm{spk}(B_k)$
for that party's public key as named in the body: \texttt{issuer\_pk},
\texttt{delegator\_pk} or \texttt{invoker\_pk}. Every body names its
signer's key, so during a scheduled rotation, when two certificates for
one identifier are valid at once, the key determines which one applies. Write $\mathrm{scope}(B_k)$ for the
body's scope expression: the granted scope for the session body, the
attenuated sub-scope for a delegation body. Finally, $\mathrm{org}(x)$ is
the \texttt{organization-id} prefix of an identifier $x$
(\cref{sec:identity-registration}); $\mathit{Root}[o]$ is the registry
root key the verifier holds for organization $o$
(\cref{sec:identity-federated}); and $\mathit{Pinned}[o]$ is the set
of policy hashes the verifier has accepted from organization $o$
(\cref{sec:policy}). The certificate kind that position $k$ requires is
$\mathrm{role}(k)$: \texttt{issuer} for $k = 0$, \texttt{agent} for
$k \ge 1$. $\mathit{self}$ is the verifier's own \texttt{service}
identifier.

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Procedure{Verify}{received chain $C$, current time $t$}
  \LComment{Phase 1 --- structure and encoding}

         \textbf{reject} if decoding fails
  \State \textbf{reject} if $N < 1$

    \State \textbf{reject} if $\Canon(B_k) \neq$ received bytes of $B_k$
           \Comment{non-canonical encoding}
  \EndFor
  \State \textbf{reject} if any $B_k.\text{kind}$ disagrees with the tag
         its position implies
  \State \textbf{reject} if $B_N.\text{aud} \neq \mathit{self}$
         \Comment{addressed to this verifier}
  \State \textbf{reject} if $H(\Canon(B_N.\text{params})) \neq
         B_N.\text{params\_hash}$
  \For{$k \gets 1$ \textbf{to} $N-1$}
    \State \textbf{reject} unless $B_k.\text{hop\_index} = k$ and
           $B_k.\text{session\_id} = B_0.\text{session\_id}$
  \EndFor
  \LComment{Phase 2 --- temporal}
  \State \textbf{reject} if $t \notin [\,B_N.\text{nbf},\,
         B_N.\text{exp}\,]$
  \For{$k \gets 1$ \textbf{to} $N$}
    \State \textbf{reject} if $B_k.\text{exp} > B_{k-1}.\text{exp}$
           \Comment{expiry must not grow along the chain}
  \EndFor
  \LComment{Phase 3 --- replay}
  \State \textbf{reject} if $(\mathrm{spk}(B_N), B_N.\text{nonce}) \in
         \textit{NonceCache}$
  \LComment{Phase 4 --- key chain consistency}
  \State \textbf{reject} if $(B_0.\text{subject\_id}, B_0.\text{subject\_pk})
         \neq (\mathrm{sid}(B_1), \mathrm{spk}(B_1))$
  \For{$k \gets 1$ \textbf{to} $N-1$}
    \State \textbf{reject} if $(B_k.\text{delegatee\_id},
           B_k.\text{delegatee\_pk}) \neq
           (\mathrm{sid}(B_{k+1}), \mathrm{spk}(B_{k+1}))$
  \EndFor
  \LComment{Phase 5 --- identity resolution}
  \For{$k \gets 0$ \textbf{to} $N$}
    \State $\mathit{cert}_k \gets
           \Call{Resolve}{\mathrm{sid}(B_k), \mathrm{spk}(B_k)}$;
           \textbf{reject} if unresolvable
    \State \textbf{reject} unless $\mathit{cert}_k$ verifies under
           $\mathit{Root}[\mathrm{org}(\mathrm{sid}(B_k))]$
    \State \textbf{reject} unless $\mathit{cert}_k.\text{registry\_id} =
           \mathrm{org}(\mathrm{sid}(B_k))$
           \Comment{registry owns the namespace}
    \State \textbf{reject} unless $\mathit{cert}_k.\text{kind} =
           \mathrm{role}(k)$
           \Comment{role fixed by position}
    \State \textbf{reject} unless $t \in [\,\mathit{cert}_k.\text{nbf},\,
           \mathit{cert}_k.\text{exp}\,]$ and
           $(\mathrm{sid}(B_k), \mathrm{spk}(B_k))$ is not revoked at $t$
    \State $\pk_k \gets \mathit{cert}_k.\text{pk}$
           \Comment{equals $\mathrm{spk}(B_k)$ by resolution}
  \EndFor

\paragraph{Structural and temporal checks.}
The verifier first decodes the received chain and confirms canonical
encoding (rejecting non-canonical inputs to prevent encoding-ambiguity
attacks), that the recomputed \texttt{params\_hash} matches
(\cref{sec:protocol-params}), that every delegation body carries its own
position as \texttt{hop\_index} and the session's
\texttt{session\_id}, and that the invocation is addressed to this verifier: the
invocation body's \texttt{aud} must equal the verifier's own
\texttt{service} identifier. It then checks that the current time is within the
invocation's validity window, that expiry times are monotonically
non-increasing along the chain (each hop's expiry cannot exceed its
parent's), and that the chain has minimum length one.

\paragraph{Replay protection.}
The verifier maintains a bounded-time nonce cache indexed by
$(\text{invoker public key}, \text{nonce})$. Chains whose key is
already present in the cache are rejected as replays. A chain is
accepted only if inserting its key into the cache succeeds, with TTL
equal to the chain's remaining validity window plus a clock-skew
tolerance. Line 13 applies no tolerance of its own: a chain is valid
exactly within $[B_N.\text{nbf}, B_N.\text{exp}]$ as the verifier's
clock reads it, and senders must allow for skew when they set those
bounds. The tolerance in the TTL has a different purpose. Instances of
one logical verifier that share the cache may disagree about the time.
The added term must be at least that disagreement, so that no instance
evicts a chain's entry while another could still accept the chain.

The lookup in phase 3 is only an early filter. What actually prevents
replay is that the final insertion is atomic and fails if the key is
already present. The lookup and the insertion are separated by identity
resolution, which may involve a network call, and by signature
verification. Without atomicity, two copies of one chain presented
concurrently would both pass phase 3 and both be accepted, on a single
instance as readily as across replicas. The nonce cache is kept per
verifier. Replay across \emph{different} verifiers is prevented not by
the cache but by the audience field: a chain names its verifier in
\texttt{aud}, and any other verifier rejects it in phase 1. The cache size
is bounded by the product of maximum verification throughput and the
maximum validity window.

The cache must be a single logical structure. A deployment that places
several verifier replicas behind a load balancer, each holding its own
independent cache, does not provide replay protection at all: the same
chain presented to two replicas is accepted twice, and an adversary who
can influence load-balancer routing can replay at will. Replicated
verifiers must therefore share a cache or partition it deterministically
by invoker public key. This is a coordination dependency on the
verification path, and \cref{thm:replay} is stated with it as an
explicit hypothesis.

\paragraph{Public key chain consistency.}
The verifier checks that each body is signed by the party its
predecessor named. The session body's subject identifier and key must
equal the signer identifier and key of $B_1$, and each delegation body's
delegatee identifier and key must equal those of the body that follows
it. Comparing identifiers as well as keys ensures that the party that
acts is the one that was named, even where one key is certified under
more than one identifier. The signer of a delegation body is its
delegator, and that of the invocation body is its invoker, so the rule
also covers the shortest chain: when $N = 1$
there are no delegations, and the orchestrator named in the session is
itself the invoker.

\paragraph{Scope monotonicity and policy compliance.}
The verifier first confirms that the session token's
\texttt{policy\_hash} is one it has pinned for the issuing organization
(\cref{sec:policy}). It then obtains the policy document, from its cache
or by hash from the issuing registry (\cref{sec:identity-resolution}),
and establishes the containment chain \cref{eq:chain-containment} using
the procedure of \cref{sec:policy-subset}: the session scope must lie
within the policy, and each hop's sub-scope within its parent's. Finally
it evaluates the invocation against the innermost scope, rejecting if
the invocation falls outside it. The pinning check is what gives the
first link its force. The issuer signs both the policy hash and the
session scope, so without pinning the verifier would be checking the
issuer against a policy the issuer had selected.

\paragraph{Approval verification.}
If policy requires approvals, the verifier finds, for each required
approval service, the receipt that names it. It resolves the service's
certificate through the same checks as phase 5, requiring kind
\texttt{approver}. It then confirms that the receipt verifies under the
certified key over the correct invocation digest, and that the current
time lies within the receipt's own validity window. A chain missing any
of these is rejected.

\paragraph{Chain signature verification.}
Finally, the verifier reconstructs $m_0, m_1, \ldots, m_N$ from the
received bodies (using position-determined tags) and confirms that they
are pairwise distinct (line 48). Distinct messages are the aggregate
variant's rogue-key precondition (\cref{sec:aggregate-variant}); the
digest recursion makes a repeat impossible short of a SHA-256 collision
in both instantiations, so for a well-formed chain the check never
fails. It then checks the chain's signatures, line 49:
$\Call{VerifyChain}{\pk_0..\pk_N,\, m_0..m_N,\, \sigma}$. For the default
instantiation, \textsc{VerifyChain} checks each $\sigma_k$ against
$(\pk_k, m_k)$ with strict Ed25519 verification. For the aggregate
variant, it is the multi-pairing check of \cref{eq:multipairing}
(\cref{sec:aggregate-variant}). If this check succeeds, all integrity,
parameter-binding, and chain provenance properties hold
simultaneously.

\paragraph{Cost.}
The measured costs of both instantiations are in
\cref{sec:eval-results}. Under the aggregate variant, the cost is well
approximated by $\alpha + \beta N$, with $\beta$ one hash to $\G{2}$
plus one Miller loop; \cref{sec:eval-results} measures it.

\Cref{fig:phases} shows why the order matters. The phases are arranged
so that the checks a hostile chain is most likely to fail are also the
cheapest to run: a malformed or expired chain is rejected on parsing and
integer comparison, and only a chain that has survived every
structural, temporal, and policy check reaches the chain-signature
verification of phase 8. An adversary who wishes to make a verifier do
the expensive work must first construct a chain that is well-formed,
unexpired, non-replayed, key-consistent, scope-monotone, and
policy-compliant --- at which point the chain signatures are the only
thing left to establish, and the work is warranted.

On a cold verifier there is one exception. Identity resolution (phase 5)
verifies each signer's certificate under its registry root before the
policy phase: $N + 1$ signature verifications, each of them a pairing
check when registry keys are BLS. Their results are cached per binding,
so an adversary naming real identities can force these checks at most
once per certified identity per cache lifetime, and at most $N + 1$ per
chain. Failed verifications are not cached, so an adversary on the
resolution path who serves invalid certificates can force one on every
attempt; deployments should authenticate or rate-limit resolution.

\subsection{Encoding}
\label{sec:protocol-encoding}

All signed structures use deterministic CBOR encoding per RFC 8949
§4.2.1, with the following additional rules. In \emph{protocol
structures} --- session, delegation and invocation bodies,
certificates, and approval receipts --- map keys are integers rather
than text strings, eliminating UTF-8 normalization considerations and
reducing encoding size. Parameter maps are the exception: they carry
names chosen by the tool author rather than by this specification, and
so use text-string keys sorted lexicographically by encoded form, per
\cref{sec:protocol-params}. The scoping is deliberate, and an
implementation that applies the integer-key rule to parameter maps will
not interoperate with one that does not. Float and tagged values are
disallowed in protocol structures except where explicitly required.
Ed25519 public keys and signatures use their RFC~8032 encodings
\citep{rfc8032}. Decoders reject non-canonical encodings and small-order
public keys, and strict verification also rejects a signature whose $R$
is of small order. The encoding of BLS group elements is part of the
aggregate variant (\cref{sec:aggregate-variant}).

Verifiers reject any structure whose received encoding differs from
the canonical re-encoding of the decoded content. This precludes a
class of attacks based on adversarially-crafted non-canonical encodings
that parse correctly but hash to different values than the
implementation expects.

\subsection{Aggregate Variant}
\label{sec:aggregate-variant}

The aggregate variant replaces the per-hop signatures with a single BLS
aggregate. Everything else in the protocol --- the digests, the bodies,
the verifier's phases 1--7 --- is unchanged; only line 49's
\textsc{VerifyChain} differs.

\paragraph{Parameters.}
The variant uses BLS12-381 \citep{ietf-pairing-curves} with the
minimal-pubkey-size variant of \citet{ietf-bls-signature}: public keys
are points in $\G{1}$ (48 bytes compressed), signatures are points in
$\G{2}$ (96 bytes compressed), and the pairing maps
$\G{1} \times \G{2}$ to $\Gt$. Hash-to-curve uses RFC 9380
\citep{rfc9380} with suite \texttt{BLS12381G2\_XMD:SHA-256\_SSWU\_RO\_}.
With signing key $\sk \in \Zq$ and public key
$\pk = \sk \cdot g_1 \in \G{1}$, a message $m$ is signed as
$\Sign(\sk, m) = \sk \cdot \HashG(m) \in \G{2}$, and verification checks
$\pair{g_1}{\sigma} \overset{?}{=} \pair{\pk}{\HashG(m)}$. Chain
signatures use the basic scheme of the CFRG draft
\citep{ietf-bls-signature}, whose ciphersuite identifier is
\begin{center}
\texttt{BLS\_SIG\_BLS12381G2\_XMD:SHA-256\_SSWU\_RO\_NUL\_}
\end{center}
(the final \texttt{NUL} marks the basic scheme).

\paragraph{The aggregate.}
The signature the chain carries is
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\text{agg}} = \sigma_0 + \sigma_1 + \cdots + \sigma_N \in \G{2},
\end{equation}
of constant size 96 bytes regardless of $N$. It is accumulated along the
chain: each participant receives the running aggregate from its
predecessor, adds its own contribution, and forwards the sum. This does
not keep the individual contributions secret --- an adversary on the
network (\cref{sec:threat-adversary}) observes successive partial sums
and recovers any $\sigma_k$ by subtraction --- and nothing in the
security argument requires that it should: \cref{thm:chain-integrity}
rests on the digest recursion, not on the secrecy of the summands. Line
49's \textsc{VerifyChain} is the multi-pairing
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:multipairing}
\pair{g_1}{\sigma_{\text{agg}}} \overset{?}{=}
    \prod_{k=0}^{N} \pair{\pk_k}{\HashG(m_k)}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Distinct messages.}
The basic scheme's aggregate verification rejects any aggregate in which
two messages are equal, and its security for distinct messages is that
of \citet{bgls2003aggregate}, which does not depend on how keys were
registered. Line 48 establishes the precondition, and the digest
recursion guarantees it by construction. Participants still register
their keys with proof-of-possession (\cref{sec:identity-pop}), for
attribution rather than for aggregate security.

\paragraph{Point validation.}
Group elements use the standard ZCash-format compressed serialization
for BLS12-381, and decoders perform subgroup membership checks on every
group element to defend against small-subgroup attacks. This check is
non-trivial on BLS12-381 and has been the source of real-world
implementation vulnerabilities; implementations should use
library-provided subgroup checks (e.g., \texttt{blst\_p1\_in\_g1})
rather than reimplementing them, and registries apply the same check to
registered public keys. The pairing-friendly-curves draft asks protocols
adopting its serialization to state three further choices
\citep{ietf-pairing-curves}. Only the compressed form is accepted, so
each point has exactly one encoding. The identity element is rejected
wherever a public key or signature is decoded: for public keys this is
the key-validation rule of the signature draft
\citep{ietf-bls-signature}, and an honest chain produces an identity
aggregate only with negligible probability. No scalar appears in any
protocol structure, since secret keys never leave their holders; a zero
secret key is excluded at registration, because its public key is the
identity.

We retain the variant because its 96-byte signature is the natural
design goal for multi-hop chains, and because \cref{sec:impl} measures
what it costs: in every configuration we measured, it is slower than the
default, and it saves only a few percent of a chain's bytes.

\section{Identity Infrastructure}
\label{sec:identity}

The cryptographic protocol of \cref{sec:protocol} assumes that
verifiers can resolve identity-to-public-key bindings for all
participants in a chain. This section describes the federated identity
infrastructure providing those bindings, including registration with
proof-of-possession, public-key resolution, key rotation, and
revocation.

\subsection{Federated Registry Model}
\label{sec:identity-federated}

DelegationChain does not assume a global root of trust. Each
participating organization operates its own \emph{registry}, which
issues identity certificates for agents, signing services, and
approval services within that organization. Each registry holds a
long-lived root keypair, with the root public key serving as the trust
anchor for all certificates the registry issues.

Two organizations establish trust through one of three mechanisms,
chosen per deployment. \emph{Direct peering} exchanges registry root
keys out-of-band as part of a business agreement; this is the simplest
model and adequate for small numbers of partners. \emph{Federated
transparency} publishes registry root keys and certificate issuances
to a shared append-only log, modeled on Certificate Transparency
\citep{rfc9162} but adapted to DelegationChain's certificate format;
verifiers trust the log's commitments and obtain inclusion proofs
binding claimed keys to log entries. \emph{Hierarchical attestation}
introduces an attestation authority signing statements binding
registry roots to organizational identifiers, analogous to a root
CA. The protocol permits all three; we recommend direct peering or
federated transparency over hierarchical attestation, the latter
inheriting the trust-concentration concerns of Web PKI.

These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive. A production deployment
may use direct peering with frequent partners and a federated log for
the long tail. Whatever the mechanism, the peering relationship is also
where the receiving organization fixes which of its partner's policies
it will honor. Its verifiers pin those policy hashes and reject
sessions naming any other (\cref{sec:policy}).

\subsection{Identifiers and Registration}
\label{sec:identity-registration}

Identifiers are structured as
\texttt{organization-id:kind:local-id}, where \texttt{kind} is one of
\texttt{agent}, \texttt{signer}, \texttt{approver}, \texttt{issuer} (the
issuance service that signs session tokens), or \texttt{service} (a
tool or MCP server acting as a verifier). The \texttt{kind} tag, which
certificates also carry, prevents confusion across roles. The verifier
enforces it rather than leaving it as a convention: each position in a
chain, and each approval receipt, must be signed under a certificate of
the matching kind (\cref{sec:protocol-verification}). A
\texttt{service} identifier names a verifier and serves only as the
audience of an invocation (\cref{sec:protocol-digest}). A verifier never
has to prove its identity in this protocol, so it needs no certificate.
The \texttt{signer} kind identifies a signing service in its own right,
for operational authentication outside this protocol, for example
between an agent runtime and the signing service that holds its key. A
signing service signs chain bodies only with the agent keys it holds,
under those agents' certificates; no position in a chain, and no
receipt, accepts a \texttt{signer} certificate. The
\texttt{organization-id} matches the issuing registry's identifier.

Registration produces a certificate binding an identifier to a public
key, signed by the registry. Certificates contain version
information, the identifier, the public key, the kind, the registry's
identifier (\texttt{registry\_id}) and public key, issuance and expiry
timestamps, a
not-before timestamp, a serial number, and the registry's signature.
Default certificate lifetimes are short --- 24 hours to 7 days for
agents and signing services, with longer lifetimes, up to a maximum the
registry publishes, permitted only in combination with active revocation
(\cref{sec:identity-revocation} uses that maximum to bound how long a
verifier must retain a revocation).
Short lifetimes reduce reliance on revocation by ensuring that
compromised credentials naturally expire within a bounded window.

\subsection{Proof of Possession}
\label{sec:identity-pop}

We require proof-of-possession (PoP) at registration for attribution. A
PoP shows that the registrant controls the secret key for the public key
it asks the registry to certify. Without it, an adversary could register
another principal's public key under its own identifier
(\threatref{5a}). The adversary would gain no ability to sign, but the
registry would then certify one key to two parties. A signature
verifying under that key would no longer identify who made it, and
that identification is what non-repudiation
(\cref{sec:threat-properties}) rests on.

DelegationChain requires every registration to include a PoP signature
over a registry-issued challenge. The challenge includes the
registrant's claimed identifier and public key, the kind tag, the
registry's identifier, a registry-issued fresh nonce (single-use,
short expiry), and a timestamp. The registrant signs the challenge's
digest with the secret key corresponding to the public key being
registered, and the registry verifies the PoP before issuing the
certificate. The registry-issued nonce prevents replay of PoPs captured
from other contexts. A registration proof must never be presentable as
a chain signature, or the reverse. Under BLS, a domain separation tag
distinct from the one used for chain signatures separates the two; under
Ed25519, which has no domain separation tags, the challenge digest's own
tag does. Registries also reject a public key that fails the scheme's
point validation: a small-order Ed25519 key, or, under the aggregate
variant, a BLS key outside the prime-order subgroup
(\cref{sec:aggregate-variant}).

Under the aggregate variant, PoP is often presented as a defense against
rogue-key attacks on BLS aggregation, and in the setting where such
attacks work --- many signers on a common message --- it is one;
\citet{ristenpart2007pop} show that a proof of possession as simple as a
signature over the certificate request suffices against rogue-key
attacks on BLS-based multisignatures. That is not our setting. An
adversary who registered a key $\pk^* = \pk' - \sum_i \pk_i$, built from
chosen targets $\pk_i$ and a key $\pk'$ it controls, could forge an
aggregate on a message the targets had all signed, and no such message
exists in a chain. Every aggregate is over pairwise distinct messages,
which is itself the rogue-key defense of the basic scheme
\citep{bgls2003aggregate,ietf-bls-signature}, so chain integrity
(\cref{thm:chain-integrity}) does not depend on PoP in either
instantiation.

\subsection{Public Key Resolution}
\label{sec:identity-resolution}

Verifiers resolve an identifier, together with the public key that the
body being checked names for it, by querying the issuing registry's
resolution endpoint. Among the certificates binding that identifier to
that key, resolution returns the most recently issued one whose validity
window contains the verification time $t$, or, if there is none, the
most recently issued one. A renewal issued in advance, or one that ends
earlier, therefore never hides an older certificate that is still valid.
Whether the binding is usable at $t$ --- within its certificate's
validity window, and not revoked --- is decided by the verifier at
line 27 of \cref{alg:verify}, not by the resolver, so line 27 still
rejects when no certificate for the binding is valid. During the overlap of a scheduled rotation
(\cref{sec:identity-rotation}) two certificates for one identifier are
valid, and the key selects between them; this applies to issuers,
agents and approval services alike. The response includes the certificate
and, if the registry is integrated with a transparency log, an
inclusion proof. The verifier validates the certificate signature under
the root public key it holds for the organization the identifier names,
that is, for the identifier's \texttt{organization-id} prefix, bound to
a root key by whichever trust mechanism of \cref{sec:identity-federated}
the two organizations use. It also requires the certificate's
\texttt{registry\_id} to equal that \texttt{organization-id}, and the
certificate's kind to be the role the identifier is used in:
\texttt{issuer} for a session token, \texttt{agent} for a delegation or
invocation, \texttt{approver} for a receipt. If an
inclusion proof is present, the verifier checks it against the log's
current signed tree head.

This binding between organization identifier and root key carries real
weight. A registry is authoritative only for identifiers in its own
organization's namespace. The naming rule of
\cref{sec:identity-registration} is enforced here, by the verifier,
rather than left as a convention. Without it, a registry the verifier
trusts for one organization could issue a certificate for another
organization's identifier: a certificate substitution
(\threatref{5d}). And because the verifier looks up pinned policies by
the organization named in the session body (\cref{sec:policy}), whoever
controlled that registry could impersonate the other organization's
issuer and grant sessions under the policies pinned for it.

Verifiers cache resolved certificates to avoid registry queries on
every verification. Cache entries are valid until the earlier of the
certificate's expiry and a configurable cache TTL (typically one
hour). Cache invalidation is event-driven for revocation: observation
of a revocation assertion immediately evicts every cached certificate
for the revoked binding.

\paragraph{Policy resolution.}
The verifier must also hold the policy that a session token's
\texttt{policy\_hash} commits to; \cref{sec:protocol-verification} checks the
session scope against it using the containment relation of
\cref{sec:policy-subset}, \cref{sec:policy-eval} then evaluates the
invocation against the innermost scope, and
\cref{thm:approval-binding} depends on that policy being one the
verifier accepted, not one the chain selected. Since the policy is written by the sending
organization and enforced by the receiving one, it has to get across the
boundary somehow, and the protocol should not leave this unsaid.

Registries host policy documents alongside certificates, retrievable by
hash: a verifier holding \texttt{policy\_hash}~$h$ requests the document
$h$ names, and accepts it only if $H(\Canon(\text{document})) = h$.
Because resolution is content-addressed, the registry is not trusted
for integrity --- it cannot substitute a different policy, only decline
to serve one --- which is the same posture the rest of
\cref{sec:identity} takes towards registries. Policies are immutable
under their hash, so a document once fetched is cached for as long as
the verifier keeps it; a policy change produces a new hash and, hence, a
new session token rather than a silent change under an existing one.

Pinning (\cref{sec:policy}) keeps this fetch off the request path.
Because a verifier accepts only hashes it has pinned, it knows every
hash it will need before any chain names one, and it can fetch and check
the documents when it pins them. A hash it has not pinned is rejected
without any fetch. A verifier that defers fetching to first use pays one
round trip on that call; \cref{sec:limitations} discusses what the
remaining dependencies cost the offline-verification claim.

\subsection{Key Rotation}
\label{sec:identity-rotation}

The protocol supports three rotation modes. \emph{Scheduled rotation}
issues a new certificate with overlapping validity windows: during the
overlap, both old and new keys are valid, ensuring that chains in
flight at the moment of rotation continue to verify. Default rotation
cadence is every 30 days for agents and every 90 days for signing
services and approval services. Because policies name approval services
by identifier rather than by key (\cref{sec:protocol-approvals}),
rotating an approval service's key needs no policy change. \emph{Emergency rotation} revokes the
prior binding (\cref{sec:identity-revocation}) and certifies a new key,
without overlap. \emph{Registry root rotation} is the most
sensitive operation: the current root signs a rotation attestation
binding the new root to the old, which is then distributed via the
same channel used for the original root. Compromised-root recovery
requires out-of-band re-bootstrap of trust and cannot be handled by
in-band rotation.

Rotation interacts with chains that are in flight when a key changes.
Under scheduled rotation the overlapping validity windows cover this
case: a chain signed under the previous key continues to verify for the
duration of the overlap, because the previous certificate is still
valid. Under emergency rotation there is no overlap, and chains signed
under the withdrawn key are rejected. The verifier always resolves the
certificate valid at \emph{verification} time. It does not attempt to
resolve the certificate that was valid when a token was signed, because
its only evidence of signing time would be a field in the token body,
signed by the very key whose compromise is at issue;
\cref{sec:identity-revocation} develops this point.

\subsection{Revocation}
\label{sec:identity-revocation}

DelegationChain prefers short-lived certificates over revocation
infrastructure: with default certificate lifetimes of 24 hours to 7
days, the maximum window during which a compromised key can be misused
is bounded by certificate expiry. Explicit revocation is provided as a
defense-in-depth mechanism for deployments choosing longer-lived
certificates.

A revocation assertion is a signed statement by a registry that a
binding --- an identifier together with a public key --- is revoked. It
revokes every certificate for that binding, and the registry must not
certify the binding again; the serial number of the certificate that
prompted it is carried for audit only. Revoking by binding rather than
by certificate matters because one binding can have several unexpired
certificates, for example after a same-key renewal, and revoking only
one of them would leave the key accepted under another. A key bound to
several identifiers must be revoked under each. A verifier accepts an
assertion only from the registry whose namespace contains the identifier
it names, as for certificates (\cref{sec:identity-resolution}). A
verifier retains a revoked binding until every certificate for it has
expired: for the registry's maximum certificate lifetime after the
revocation, plus a bound on clock skew. Verifiers learn of assertions through polling (default
interval 60 seconds), push notification, or transparency log
monitoring; the choice is a deployment decision. The end-to-end window
between key compromise and unforgeability is bounded by the sum of
detection latency, reporting latency, assertion issuance,
propagation, and residual cache time, of which only the propagation and
cache terms are bounded by configuration (typically below 120
seconds). Detection and reporting latency are unbounded by the
protocol: they depend on the deploying organization's monitoring, and
nothing in the design constrains them. The property claimed in
\cref{sec:threat-properties} is accordingly bounded revocation
\emph{propagation}, not bounded exposure.

Revocation applies relative to \emph{verification time}. A binding
revoked at time $t_r$ is not accepted by any verifier that has learned
of the revocation, including for chains whose tokens were signed before
$t_r$. The gentler alternative --- continuing to honor chains signed
before $t_r$, so that revocation does not retroactively invalidate work
already in progress --- is not available to us. A verifier's only
evidence of signing time is a timestamp in the token body, and that
timestamp is signed by the key whose compromise prompted the
revocation. An adversary holding a stolen key, observing the
revocation, can set the field to any earlier value and produce a chain
that verifies against the pre-revocation certificate. A revocation rule
resting on that field can be defeated by backdating, which would leave
the mechanism without force.

We therefore accept the operational cost. Chains in flight when a
revocation is issued may fail verification and must be reconstructed
under a rotated key. Two things bound the exposure: only chains
outstanding within the propagation window are affected, and scheduled
rotation (\cref{sec:identity-rotation}) avoids the situation entirely by
overlapping the validity windows of the old and new certificates, so
that routine key changes never invalidate work in flight. Emergency
rotation, by construction, does.

\section{Policy Expression Language}
\label{sec:policy}

This section introduces the policy expression language used to describe
agent authorization scopes throughout DelegationChain. The language
serves two roles, and separating them is worth doing at the outset,
because the verifier treats them differently.

A \emph{policy} is a scope expression published by an organization for
its agents and committed to by the \texttt{policy\_hash} field of the
session token (\cref{sec:protocol-digest}). It is a ceiling: the outer
bound on what any agent operating under that session may do, fixed by
the organizations involved rather than by any agent. The session names
which policy applies; the verifier decides which policies it will
accept at all, as described below, and resolves the document from the
registry by hash (\cref{sec:identity-resolution}). A \emph{scope} is a scope expression
carried inside a token --- the authority the issuer granted the
orchestrator, in the session token, and the attenuated sub-scope each
delegator passed on, in a delegation token.

Both are drawn from the same grammar, and a single relation connects
them. A chain is accepted only if
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:chain-containment}
S \supseteq \mathrm{scope}(B_0) \supseteq \mathrm{scope}(B_1)
  \supseteq \cdots \supseteq \mathrm{scope}(B_{N-1}),
\end{equation}
where $S$ is the resolved policy and $B_0, \ldots, B_{N-1}$ are the
session and delegation bodies. Every link is checked by the containment
procedure of \cref{sec:policy-subset}; the verifier then evaluates the
invocation against $\mathrm{scope}(B_{N-1})$, the innermost scope.

The first link needs care, because the issuer signs both
\texttt{policy\_hash} and $\mathrm{scope}(B_0)$ in the same body. If the
verifier accepted whatever hash a session named, the check would compare
the issuer against a policy the issuer had chosen. Content addressing
prevents a different document from being substituted behind a hash, but
it does nothing about which hash is selected. The verifier therefore
accepts only policies it has \emph{pinned} for the issuing organization:
a set of policy hashes fixed in its own configuration, or in the peering
agreement with that partner (\cref{sec:identity-federated}). A session
naming any other hash is rejected. The ceiling $S$ is then drawn from a
set the receiving organization agreed to, and the issuer can do no more
than choose among policies the verifier has already accepted.

Pinning also closes a gap the protocol would otherwise leave: the
receiving organization had no way to state its own terms, only to
enforce the sending organization's. A pinned policy may be one the
sending organization published and the receiving organization reviewed,
or one the two wrote together for the relationship. Either way, it is
never one the receiving organization has not seen. What pinning does not
defend against is the verifier's own configuration. We treat the pinned
set as part of the verifier's trust base (\cref{sec:security-assumptions}).

With pinning in place, every link of \cref{eq:chain-containment} belongs
to the scope monotonicity property of \cref{sec:threat-properties}, and
\cref{thm:scope-monotonicity} states it for the whole chain.

One consequence carries the weight of \cref{thm:approval-binding}.
\Cref{def:subset} requires a contained scope's approval requirement to
be at least as restrictive as its container's, so approval requirements
can only strengthen as one moves right along
\cref{eq:chain-containment}. An approval requirement written into the
policy therefore cannot be weakened by the issuer, nor by any agent
further down the chain. This is what lets the verifier evaluate against
$\mathrm{scope}(B_{N-1})$ --- data the agents wrote --- and still be
certain the requirement it obtains is no weaker than the one in a
policy it has itself accepted.

The design of the policy language is constrained by three requirements
arising from its role in the protocol. First, the verifier must be able
to check cheaply, given two scope expressions $S_1$ and $S_2$
purportedly in a delegation relationship, whether $S_2 \subseteq S_1$ ---
the scope monotonicity check of \cref{sec:protocol-verification} ---
erring towards rejection where it cannot establish containment. Second,
evaluation of an invocation against a scope must terminate in time
bounded by the size of the scope expression and the invocation, with
no possibility of unbounded computation. Third, scope expressions must
be free of side effects: evaluation must not perform network calls,
file I/O, or other operations that could be exploited for information
exfiltration or denial of service.

These requirements rule out general-purpose policy languages such as
Rego or Cedar, which are powerful but operationally expensive to reason
about and historically have included features (e.g., HTTP fetches in
some versions of Rego) that violate the side-effect-free requirement.
DelegationChain's policy language is deliberately restricted to a
fragment that supports realistic enterprise authorization conditions
while admitting straightforward decidability and termination analyses.

\subsection{Grammar}
\label{sec:policy-grammar}

The grammar of scope expressions, in Backus-Naur form:

\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
scope    ::= rule+ | "allow" "all" | "deny" "all"
rule     ::= "allow" at tool action params? where? approval?
at       ::= "at" "=" principal
tool     ::= "tool" "=" identifier
action   ::= "action" "=" identifier
params   ::= "params" "{" binding ("," binding)* "}"
binding  ::= path ":" type
where    ::= "where" expr
expr     ::= expr "and" expr
           | "(" expr ")"
           | atom
atom     ::= path numop value
           | path strop string-value
           | path "in" list-value
           | path "under" string-value
approval ::= "approval" "requires" principal ("," principal)*
type     ::= "int" | "string" | "bool"
numop    ::= "<" | "<=" | "==" | ">=" | ">"
strop    ::= "==" | "starts_with" | "ends_with" | "contains"
path     ::= identifier ("." identifier)*
principal  ::= identifier ":" identifier ":" identifier
identifier ::= letter (letter | digit | "_" | "-")*
value    ::= integer | string | boolean
list-value ::= "[" value ("," value)* "]"
string-value ::= string
\end{lstlisting}

A scope is either a special form (\texttt{allow all}, \texttt{deny all})
or a non-empty disjunction of \emph{rules}. Each rule specifies the
verifier it authorizes (\texttt{at}), a tool, an action, an optional
declaration of expected parameter types
(\texttt{params}), an optional condition on parameter values
(\texttt{where}), and an optional approval requirement
(\texttt{approval}). A \emph{principal} is a registry identifier of the
form \texttt{organization-id:kind:local-id} (\cref{sec:identity-registration});
its kind must be \texttt{service} in an \texttt{at} clause and
\texttt{approver} in an \texttt{approval} clause, and a policy that
breaks either rule is malformed. The semantics of a scope is the union of the
invocations permitted by its rules; \texttt{deny all} permits nothing,
and \texttt{allow all} is a reserved form used for top-level trusted
contexts (e.g., the issuer's session-issuance authority). It permits
every audience as well as every tool and action, so the mandatory
audience clause does not constrain it. A verifier that pins
\texttt{allow all} for a partner accepts that partner's sessions for
anything addressed to it, with no policy limiting them, so it is not
meant to be pinned across organizations.

The condition language inside \texttt{where} clauses is
\emph{conjunctive}: a clause is a conjunction of atomic predicates over
parameter values. Each atomic predicate is one of four forms --- numeric
comparison, string operator, list membership (\texttt{in}), or
path-prefix containment (\texttt{under}). No predicate involves function
calls, network operations, or unbounded iteration.

The three parameter types are disjoint at the value level. Each
corresponds to a different CBOR major type in the parameter encoding of
\cref{sec:protocol-params} --- integers, text strings, booleans --- so
every parameter value has at most one type, and a leaf value of any
other encoded type (a byte string, null or an array) matches no
declaration. The disjointness matters. If two types shared values, two
rules declaring the same parameter under different types could both
match one invocation, and the containment procedure of
\cref{sec:policy-subset} would be unsound; a type for filesystem paths,
whose values are text strings, would do exactly that. Path-prefix
containment is therefore an operator on strings rather than a type.
\texttt{p under q} holds when the value of string parameter
\texttt{p} is a canonical absolute path and the segments of \texttt{q}
are a prefix of its segments, equality included. A canonical absolute
path starts with \texttt{/}; it is either \texttt{/} alone or
\texttt{/} followed by non-empty segments joined by \texttt{/}; no
segment is \texttt{.} or \texttt{..}; and there is no trailing
\texttt{/}. A non-canonical value makes the atom false. The canonical-form condition is not optional:
without it, \texttt{/home/agent/workspace/../../etc/passwd} would pass
a check for \texttt{under "/home/agent/workspace"}. The literal
\texttt{q} must itself be a canonical absolute path. A policy containing
a non-canonical one is malformed and rejected when it is loaded, since
such a prefix would otherwise silently match nothing.

\paragraph{Lexical details.}
Whitespace is insignificant. Strings are double-quoted, with JSON
escapes. Integers are decimal, with an optional leading \texttt{-}, and
lie in the CBOR integer range $[-2^{64}, 2^{64}-1]$. Booleans are
\texttt{true} and \texttt{false}. In identifiers, \texttt{letter} is
\texttt{[A-Za-z]} and \texttt{digit} is \texttt{[0-9]}, which rules out
Unicode confusables and makes normalization irrelevant for identifiers.
String literals are NFC-normalized, like the text values they are
compared with (\cref{sec:protocol-params}), and the string operators
compare bytewise.

\paragraph{Well-formedness.}
The grammar admits scopes that make no sense, and the procedures of
\cref{sec:policy-eval,sec:policy-subset} are defined only for
well-formed ones. A rule is \emph{well-formed} when three conditions
hold.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Every path its \texttt{where} clause mentions is declared in
          its \texttt{params}. No path is declared twice, and no
          declared path is a strict prefix of another (\texttt{a}
          together with \texttt{a.b}).
    \item Every atom pairs its operator, the declared type of its path
          and its operand as in the table below. Any other combination
          is malformed.
    \item The principal in its \texttt{at} clause has kind
          \texttt{service}, and those in its \texttt{approval} clause
          have kind \texttt{approver}.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Operator & Path type & Operand \\
\midrule
\texttt{<} \ \texttt{<=} \ \texttt{>=} \ \texttt{>} & \texttt{int} & integer \\
\texttt{==} & any & a literal of the path's type \\
\texttt{starts\_with} \ \texttt{ends\_with} \ \texttt{contains}
            & \texttt{string} & string \\
\texttt{in} & any & a non-empty list, every element of the path's type \\
\texttt{under} & \texttt{string} & a canonical absolute path \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The operator \texttt{==} appears in both \texttt{numop} and
\texttt{strop}; the declared type of the path decides which is meant. A
scope is well-formed when all of its rules are, and a scope that
violates any of these conditions is \emph{malformed}.
\textsc{LoadPolicy} rejects a malformed policy. A malformed scope inside
a session or delegation body makes that body malformed, and the chain is
rejected when it is decoded, at line 2 of \cref{alg:verify}. The
containment procedure of \cref{sec:policy-subset} also returns false on a
malformed scope, as a defensive check that a decoded chain never
reaches. Implementations also bound the size of a scope --- its rules,
parameters, atoms, list lengths, text lengths and path depth --- so that
evaluation and containment stay bounded on input the sender chooses;
those bounds belong in a normative specification.
One consequence is used in \cref{prop:decidability}. In a well-formed
rule, every path the clause mentions is declared, so it is present in
any invocation that passes the rule's closed-world check, and step 1(c)
of the evaluator cannot fail.

Disjunction and negation are deliberately absent from the expression
language. Disjunction is recovered one level up: a scope is a union of
rules, so a condition $a \lor b$ is written as two rules, and the
expressive loss is close to nil. Negation is recovered only in part.
Numeric comparisons have complements within the language --- the
negation of $x > c$ is $x \le c$ --- but the string, membership, and
path-prefix predicates do not, so a condition such as ``the path is
\emph{not} under \texttt{/etc}'' cannot be written. We accept this.
Authorization policies are more naturally written as enumerations of
what is permitted than as exclusions of what is not, and the restriction
is what buys the containment procedure of \cref{sec:policy-subset} its
polynomial running time: implication between arbitrary propositional
formulas is coNP-complete, and a verifier is not a good place to run a
SAT solver on an input the sender chose.

\subsection{Example}
\label{sec:policy-example}

A representative policy fragment governing a payments tool:

\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
allow at=orgb:service:payments
  tool=payments action=transfer
  params { amount: int,
           to_account: string }
  where amount <= 1000
allow at=orgb:service:payments
  tool=payments action=transfer
  params { amount: int,
           to_account: string }
  where amount <= 100000
        and to_account in
            ["acct_vendor_a", "acct_vendor_b"]
  approval requires orga:approver:finance
allow at=orgb:service:files
  tool=files action=read
  params { file: string }
  where file under "/home/agent/workspace"
\end{lstlisting}

This policy permits, at two named services of Organization B,
unconditional transfers up to 1{,}000 units; transfers up to 100{,}000
units to two approved vendors with finance approval; and read access to
files anywhere under a specific workspace directory. Any invocation falling outside these three rules is denied
by the implicit closing rule \texttt{deny all}.

\subsection{Evaluation Semantics}
\label{sec:policy-eval}

Given a scope $S$ and an invocation
$I = (\text{aud}, \text{tool}, \text{action}, \text{params})$, whose
audience is the \texttt{aud} field of the invocation body, the evaluator returns
\textsc{allow}, \textsc{allow-with-approval}(\textit{services}), or
\textsc{deny}. The special forms are decided directly: \texttt{allow all}
returns \textsc{allow} and \texttt{deny all} returns \textsc{deny}.
Otherwise $S$ is a list of rules, and the procedure is:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item For each rule $r$ in $S$ in declaration order:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item If $r$'s audience, tool, or action does not match $I$,
              continue.
        \item Type-check $I.\text{params}$ against $r$'s
              \texttt{params} declaration. The check is
              \emph{closed-world}: the rule matches only if
              $\mathrm{dom}(I.\text{params})$ is exactly the set of
              parameter paths $r$ declares, and every value has its
              declared type. If a declared parameter is missing, if any
              parameter has the wrong type, or if the invocation carries
              a parameter $r$ does not declare, continue.
        \item Resolve each path mentioned in $r$'s \texttt{where}
              clause against $I.\text{params}$. If any path resolves
              to absent, continue (treating the rule as inapplicable).
        \item Evaluate $r$'s \texttt{where} clause. If false, continue.
        \item If $r$ has no approval requirement, return \textsc{allow}.
              Otherwise return \textsc{allow-with-approval}($r$'s
              required approval services).
    \end{enumerate}
    \item If no rule matched, return \textsc{deny}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The closed-world condition.}
Step 1(b) is closed-world by design: an invocation carrying a parameter
that the rule does not declare matches no rule, and is denied. The
alternative --- checking declared parameters and ignoring the rest ---
would leave undeclared parameters cryptographically bound but never
authorized. Under \cref{sec:protocol-params} every supplied value sits
inside $\Canon(P)$ and is therefore signed and non-repudiable, but
binding is not constraint. A rule declaring \texttt{amount} and
\texttt{to\_account} says nothing about a third parameter
\texttt{override\_limits}, and a verifier that accepted an invocation
carrying it would be honoring an authorization the policy never
granted. Closing the world is what makes a parameter declaration a
constraint rather than a schema hint.

\paragraph{The audience clause.}
Every rule names the verifier it authorizes, and step 1(a) requires that
name to equal the invocation's \texttt{aud}. The clause is mandatory for
the same reason the parameter check is closed-world. The \texttt{aud}
field of \cref{sec:protocol-digest} binds an invocation to one verifier,
but binding is not constraint. Without the clause, \texttt{tool=payments}
would authorize payments at every organization that pinned the policy.
A prompt-injected agent could then address a fresh, correctly signed
chain to the wrong one and remain within policy. A policy used with
several partners therefore lists its rules per verifier.

\paragraph{Match order.}
Rules are tried in declaration order and the first match wins, so a
broad early rule silently suppresses the approval requirement of a
narrower rule later in the scope. In the policy of
\cref{sec:policy-example} a 500-unit transfer to
\texttt{acct\_vendor\_a} matches rule 1 and returns \textsc{allow}
without ever reaching rule 2's finance approval. This is a
policy-authoring hazard rather than a protocol defect --- the outcome is
exactly what the written policy says --- but it is easy to introduce by
accident, and authors should order rules from most restrictive to least,
or scope early rules narrowly enough that they cannot shadow a later
approval requirement.

The evaluator is deterministic, has no side effects, and contains no
loops over data of unbounded size: the outer loop iterates over rules
(a finite syntactic component of $S$), and \texttt{where} clause
evaluation is a finite recursive descent over the expression tree.

\begin{proposition}[Termination]
\label{prop:termination}
Evaluation of an invocation against a scope $S$ terminates in time
$O(|S| \cdot |\text{params}|)$, where $|S|$ is the syntactic size of the
scope expression and $|\text{params}|$ is the total size of the
invocation's parameter encoding.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The outer loop iterates over rules of $S$, of which there are at most
$|S|$. For each rule, parameter resolution traverses the parameter map,
which is bounded by $|\text{params}|$; \texttt{where} expression
evaluation is a recursive descent whose depth is bounded by the
syntactic depth of the expression, itself bounded by $|S|$. The total
work is bounded by the product, yielding the claimed bound.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Subset Relation}
\label{sec:policy-subset}

The verifier of \cref{sec:protocol-verification} must check that the
sub-scope at each delegation hop is a subset of its parent's scope.
This is a check over scope expressions, performed once per hop at
verification time, and not a runtime check over invocations. We define
the relation semantically and then give a syntactic procedure that
soundly under-approximates it.

\begin{definition}[Subset relation]
\label{def:subset}
Given scope expressions $S_1, S_2$, we say $S_2 \subseteq S_1$ iff every
invocation $I$ that the evaluator accepts under $S_2$ is also accepted
under $S_1$, with at least as restrictive an approval requirement.
\end{definition}

Containment is checked --- conservatively, as \cref{prop:decidability}
and \cref{rem:incompleteness} make precise --- by the following
procedure, which first returns false if either scope is malformed
(\cref{sec:policy-grammar}):

\begin{enumerate}
    \item If $S_1$ is \texttt{allow all}, return true.
    \item If $S_2$ is \texttt{deny all}, return true. If $S_2$ is
          \texttt{allow all} (and, by step 1, $S_1$ is not), return
          false.
    \item For each rule $r_2$ in $S_2$:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Find the first rule $r_1$ of $S_1$, in declaration
              order, such that $r_2 \subseteq r_1$ as rules. If there is
              none, return false.
        \item Consider every rule $r'$ that precedes $r_1$ in $S_1$,
              agrees with $r_2$ on audience, tool, action, and parameter
              declaration, and has a \texttt{where} clause jointly
              satisfiable with $r_2$'s. Skip $r'$ if some rule $r_2'$
              preceding $r_2$ in $S_2$ agrees with $r'$ on audience,
              tool, action and declaration and has a clause implied by
              $r'$'s. Otherwise check that $r'$'s approval requirement
              is no stronger than $r_2$'s, and return false if it is
              stronger.
    \end{enumerate}
    \item Otherwise, return true.
\end{enumerate}

Step 3(b) is needed because rules are matched in declaration order
(\cref{sec:policy-eval}). An invocation accepted under $S_2$ is
evaluated under $S_1$ against the \emph{first} rule of $S_1$ that
matches it. Since $r_1$ matches every invocation $r_2$ does, that first
rule is $r_1$ or one of the rules before it. Rules after $r_1$ never
decide such an invocation, and step 3(b) does not examine them. Without
the check, a child scope could quietly drop an approval requirement
imposed by an earlier parent rule --- exactly the escalation
\cref{def:subset} is meant to exclude --- and the procedure would not
notice.

The skip condition handles the converse case, in which a rule of $S_2$
is itself shadowed. An invocation that $r_2$ decides under $S_2$ matched
no earlier rule of $S_2$, and in particular did not match $r_2'$.
Suppose it matched $r'$. It would then pass $r_2'$'s closed-world check,
since the declarations are identical; every path in $r_2'$'s clause
would be present, since in a well-formed rule those paths are declared;
and it would satisfy that clause, which $r'$'s implies. So it would
match $r_2'$, which it did not. Hence $r'$ cannot be the rule that
decides it under $S_1$.
Without the skip, the procedure would reject policies written in the
order \cref{sec:policy-eval} recommends. With an approval-requiring rule
placed before an overlapping permissive one, such a policy would fail to
contain itself. Phase 6 would then reject every session whose scope is
the pinned policy, and every agent that re-delegates its whole scope
unchanged.

With both conditions the procedure is reflexive: $P \subseteq P$ for
every scope $P$. For each rule $r_2$ of $P$, step 3(a) succeeds, since
$r_2$ subsumes itself, and the $r_1$ it finds lies at or before $r_2$.
Every rule $r'$ preceding $r_1$ is therefore an earlier rule of $P$
itself, and it is skipped with $r_2' = r'$. The incompleteness of
\cref{rem:incompleteness}, which concerns rules covered only by unions
of other rules, remains.

Rule subsumption ($r_2 \subseteq r_1$) requires: the audiences, tools
and actions match; $r_1$ and $r_2$ declare the \emph{same} set of parameter paths
with the same types; $r_1$'s approval requirement is no stronger than
$r_2$'s (so that any approvals satisfying $r_2$ also satisfy $r_1$);
and $r_2$'s \texttt{where} clause implies $r_1$'s.

The identity condition on parameter declarations follows from the
closed-world rule of \cref{sec:policy-eval} together with the
disjointness of types (\cref{sec:policy-grammar}). A rule matches only
invocations whose parameter set is exactly what it declares and whose
values have exactly the declared types, and each value has at most one
type. The declaration of any rule matching an invocation $I$ is
therefore determined by $I$ alone, and two rules with different
declarations --- different parameter sets, or the same parameters under
different types --- accept disjoint sets of invocations:
declaring fewer parameters is strictly more restrictive rather than more
permissive, and no containment holds in either direction. Requiring
identical declarations is therefore the only sound relation available
without further machinery, such as an explicit notion of optional
parameters that the language does not currently have.

The remaining sub-problem is deciding implication between \texttt{where}
clauses. Because a clause is a conjunction of atoms
(\cref{sec:policy-grammar}), this reduces to a check over atoms rather
than to satisfiability. The atoms have a restricted structure: numeric
comparisons are over a fixed integer path and constants, string
operators are over fixed string paths and constant patterns, list
membership is over fixed lists, and path-prefix containment is over
fixed prefixes. Atoms over distinct paths are independent, so a
conjunction $C_2 = \bigwedge_j a_j$ implies a conjunction
$C_1 = \bigwedge_i b_i$ exactly when, for each $b_i$, those atoms of
$C_2$ over the same path as $b_i$ jointly imply $b_i$. Joint
satisfiability, needed by step 3(b), decomposes the same way: a
conjunction is unsatisfiable exactly when the constraints induced on
some path are inconsistent.

The per-path checks are exact for integer and boolean paths, and for
string paths whose value is confined to a finite set by \texttt{==} or
\texttt{in}. There the atoms induce an interval, a finite set, or both,
and implication and satisfiability are decided by inspecting them. For
string paths without such a set --- constraints that combine
\texttt{starts\_with}, \texttt{ends\_with}, \texttt{contains} and
\texttt{under} --- we do not have an exact procedure, and the checks are
sound but incomplete. Implication is reported only when a single
constraint already forces the atom (a longer required prefix implies a
shorter one, for instance). A conjunction is reported unsatisfiable only
when two of its constraints are directly incompatible (two required
prefixes, neither a prefix of the other, for instance). Each check
therefore errs only in the direction containment can tolerate. A missed
implication stops step 3(a) or the skip condition from applying, and a
missed contradiction makes step 3(b) examine a rule it could have
ignored; either way the result can only be a rejection. An atom on a path
the other clause does not constrain is judged against the unconstrained
domain of the path's declared type. That is sound because, by
well-formedness, every path a clause mentions is declared, and the rules
being compared have identical declarations. No search over truth
assignments is required at any point.

\begin{proposition}[Soundness and cost of the containment procedure]
\label{prop:decidability}
The procedure above terminates in time polynomial in $|S_1| + |S_2|$,
and whenever it returns true, $S_2 \subseteq S_1$ in the sense of
\cref{def:subset}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\emph{Cost.} The procedure enumerates pairs
$(r_2, r_1) \in S_2 \times S_1$, of which there are at most
$|S_1| \cdot |S_2|$. Each pair check compares two identifiers, compares
two parameter declarations for equality, compares two approval
requirements, and decides implication between two conjunctions of
atoms. The last of these groups the atoms of each clause by path and,
for each atom of $r_1$'s clause, evaluates an implication check against
the constraint that $r_2$'s atoms induce on the same path. The number of
such checks is bounded by the product of the two atom counts, and each
takes time polynomial in the sizes of the atoms involved: linear in the
literals for the string checks, and in the list lengths for
membership. Step 3(b) performs a
satisfiability check of the same form over the same pairs, and its skip
condition adds, for each $r_2$, at most one implication check per pair
of a rule of $S_1$ and a rule of $S_2$. The total is polynomial in
$|S_1| + |S_2|$.

\emph{Soundness.} Steps 1 and 2 return true only when $S_1$ accepts
every invocation with no approval requirement or $S_2$ accepts none, and
containment then holds trivially. Otherwise $S_2$ is a list of rules.
Suppose the procedure returns true, and let $I$ be an
invocation the evaluator accepts under $S_2$, decided there by the first
rule $r_2 \in S_2$ that matches it. Step 3(a) exhibited the first
$r_1 \in S_1$ with $r_2 \subseteq r_1$. Then $r_1$ matches $I$ as well:
the audience, tool and action agree, so step 1(a) of
\cref{sec:policy-eval} passes; the parameter
declarations are identical, so the closed-world check of step 1(b)
passes; $r_1$ is well-formed (\cref{sec:policy-grammar}), so every path
its clause mentions is declared and therefore present, and step 1(c)
passes; and $r_2$'s clause holds of $I$ and
implies $r_1$'s, so step 1(d) passes. Hence $I$ is accepted under $S_1$.
For the approval component, the rule that actually decides $I$ under
$S_1$ is the first rule of $S_1$ matching $I$. Because $r_1$ matches
$I$, that rule is either $r_1$ itself, whose approval requirement step
3(a) compared with $r_2$'s, or a rule $r'$ preceding $r_1$. In the
second case $r'$ and $r_2$ both match $I$, so by the identity argument
above they have identical declarations. They also agree on audience,
tool and action, and $I$ satisfies both clauses, so those clauses are
jointly satisfiable. Nor was $r'$ skipped. A skipping rule $r_2'$ would
precede $r_2$ in $S_2$, agree with $r'$ on audience, tool, action and
declaration, and have a clause implied by $r'$'s. So $I$, which matches
$r'$, would match $r_2'$ too --- it passes the same closed-world check,
every path in $r_2'$'s clause is declared by well-formedness and so
present, and that clause is implied by $r'$'s --- contradicting the choice of $r_2$ as the
first rule of $S_2$ matching $I$. Step 3(b) therefore examined
$r'$, and its approval requirement is no stronger than $r_2$'s. Thus $I$ is
accepted under $S_1$ with an approval requirement no stronger than the
one $S_2$ imposes, which is \cref{def:subset}.
\end{proof}

Measured, \textsc{Contains} takes 9.0~\textmu{}s in the worst case for
scopes of 4 rules with 4 atoms each, and 5146~\textmu{}s at 64 rules
with 8 atoms each (\cref{sec:eval-results}). Because the procedure's
cost grows with the product of the two scopes' sizes, a verifier should
cache containment results by scope digest, as the prefix cache of
\cref{sec:eval-results} does.

\begin{remark}[Incompleteness]
\label{rem:incompleteness}
The procedure is sound but not complete. Step 3(a) requires each rule of
$S_2$ to be subsumed by a \emph{single} rule of $S_1$, so a child rule
covered only by the union of several parent rules is rejected. Take
$S_1$ to be the two rules
\texttt{allow at=v tool=t action=a params \{x: int\} where x <= 10} and
\texttt{allow at=v tool=t action=a params \{x: int\} where x > 10}, and
$S_2$ the single rule
\texttt{allow at=v tool=t action=a params \{x: int\}}. Every
invocation accepted under $S_2$ is accepted under $S_1$, so
$S_2 \subseteq S_1$ holds semantically; but neither rule of $S_1$
subsumes $S_2$'s rule on its own, and the procedure returns false. The
incompleteness is conservative --- the procedure rejects some legitimate
delegations and accepts no illegitimate ones --- and we prefer that
direction of error. Deciding \cref{def:subset} exactly would require
reasoning about unions of rules, which the per-rule structure of the
procedure does not support; we leave it open whether the exact relation
admits an efficient decision procedure for this language. String
constraints are a second, independent source of incompleteness: for
string paths without a finite set of admissible values, the per-path
implication and satisfiability checks are themselves incomplete
(\cref{sec:policy-subset}), in the same conservative direction. Two
further sources come from the shape of the procedure. First, a child
rule that can never decide an invocation --- because its clause is
unsatisfiable, or because earlier child rules catch everything it
matches --- must still be subsumed by some parent rule. It is rejected
if no parent rule with the same audience, tool, action and declaration
has an approval requirement weak enough, although the rule is harmless.
Second, the skip test of step 3(b) asks that the whole of $r'$'s clause
imply the clause of a single earlier child rule. It would be enough that
every invocation matched by both $r'$ and $r_2$ is decided by earlier
child rules taken together. Both are conservative. Skipping child rules
with unsatisfiable clauses in step 3(a) would be sound, and is left to
future work.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Limitations of the Policy Language}
\label{sec:policy-limitations}

The policy language is deliberately bounded. It cannot express
semantic conditions of the form ``is this email message
professional?'' or ``is this code review constructive?'' that would
require external evaluation, typically by another LLM. Such conditions
are non-deterministic and admit no obvious decidability or termination
guarantee, and we judge them inappropriate for inclusion in
authorization policies that must be cryptographically committed to via
\texttt{policy\_hash}.

The language also cannot express conditions referring to state outside
the invocation itself: account balances, current time relative to
business hours, counts of prior invocations within a window. Such
conditions either require stateful evaluation (which would violate the
side-effect-free requirement) or external queries (which would
introduce latency and additional trust dependencies). Where stateful
authorization is required, we anticipate it being handled at the tool
implementation layer, after the protocol's stateless authorization
check has succeeded.

The \texttt{under} operator is lexical. Its canonical-form requirement
stops \texttt{..} traversal, but it cannot see the filesystem: a
symbolic link inside the permitted tree can point outside it. A tool
that accepts a path authorized by \texttt{under} must therefore resolve
it without following links out of the tree, and the protocol's check
does not relieve it of that.

The closed-world condition of \cref{sec:policy-eval} carries an
operational cost of its own. Adding an optional parameter to a tool is
not a backward-compatible change: every rule governing that tool must be
updated to declare the new parameter before any invocation may supply
it, and until the updated policy has propagated, invocations carrying
the new parameter are denied. Because policies are committed to by hash
in session tokens, that update is a policy version change rather than an
edit in place, and every partner whose verifiers accept the policy
must pin the new hash before sessions under it will verify. We consider this the right default, since the
failure mode is denial rather than unauthorized acceptance, but
deployments whose tool schemas change often should expect policy churn
in proportion. The audience clause of \cref{sec:policy-eval} has the
same kind of cost. Adding a verifier, such as a new region or a staging
instance, means adding rules that name it. That is a new policy version,
which every partner pinning the policy must re-pin, including partners
the new verifier does not affect. This is the re-pinning that
\cref{sec:protocol-approvals} avoids for approval services by naming
them by identifier. For audiences we accept it, since which verifiers a
policy authorizes is part of what the partners agreed to. An
organization can confine the churn by publishing a separate policy for
each partner.

These limitations are deliberate. A more expressive language would
provide more flexibility but at the cost of guarantees we consider
essential. We discuss possible extensions to semantic conditions via
verifiable LLM attestation in \cref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Security Analysis}
\label{sec:security}

This section presents the security argument for DelegationChain. We
state the protocol's security properties as theorems, reduce each to
standard cryptographic assumptions or to the properties of the policy
language, and sketch proofs. The arguments here are informal in the
sense that they have not been mechanized in a proof assistant or
verified using symbolic protocol analyzers such as Tamarin or
ProVerif. We discuss the planned formalization in
\cref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Assumptions}
\label{sec:security-assumptions}

The security arguments below rest on the following assumptions:

\begin{description}
    \item[\textbf{Signature unforgeability.}] The signature scheme
    $\Sigma$ is existentially unforgeable under chosen-message attack
    (EUF-CMA). For the default instantiation, Ed25519 with strict
    verification, \citet{brendel2021ed25519} prove this, and strong
    unforgeability besides. For the aggregate variant, the BLS scheme
    \citep{boneh2001bls,ietf-bls-signature} on BLS12-381
    \citep{ietf-pairing-curves} is EUF-CMA under the co-computational
    Diffie-Hellman assumption on the curve, in the random oracle model
    for $\HashG$.
    \item[\textbf{Aggregate security.}] For the aggregate variant
    only (\cref{sec:aggregate-variant}): aggregate signatures over
    pairwise distinct messages are existentially unforgeable
    \citep{bgls2003aggregate}. No PPT adversary can produce a tuple
    $(\pk_1, \ldots, \pk_n, m_1, \ldots, m_n, \sigma_{\text{agg}})$
    with the $m_i$ pairwise distinct such that the multi-pairing check
    accepts, unless it has obtained valid individual signatures on each
    $m_i$ from the holder of $\sk_i$. The result holds under the same
    assumptions as BLS unforgeability. It places no requirement on how
    the $\pk_i$ were registered, nor on their being distinct.
    \Cref{sec:protocol-digest} guarantees distinct messages, and phase 8
    of \cref{alg:verify2} checks it.
    \item[\textbf{Collision resistance of SHA-256.}] SHA-256 is
    collision-resistant in the standard sense.
    \item[\textbf{Honest signing key custody.}] Signing keys remain in
    their designated hardware security modules and are not exfiltrated.
    Compromise of an HSM is explicitly out of scope per
    \cref{sec:threat-outside}.
    \item[\textbf{Honest registry root.}] Registry root signing keys
    are honestly managed. Compromise of a root is addressed via
    rotation and detection (\cref{sec:identity-rotation}); the
    formal arguments below assume an honest root, with the implication
    that root compromise reduces security to a recovery problem rather
    than a forgery problem.
    \item[\textbf{Verifier configuration integrity.}] The set of policy
    hashes a verifier has pinned for each partner, and the root key it
    associates with each partner organization, reflect its own
    organization's decisions and cannot be written by the adversary.
    The first link of \cref{eq:chain-containment}, and through it
    \cref{thm:scope-monotonicity,thm:approval-binding}, is only as good
    as that configuration.
\end{description}

\subsection{Theorems}
\label{sec:security-theorems}

\begin{theorem}[Token unforgeability]
\label{thm:unforgeability}
Under the unforgeability of $\Sigma$, no PPT adversary can produce a
session, delegation, or invocation token that verifies under a public
key $\pk$ for which it does not hold the corresponding $\sk$, except
with negligible probability.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
A valid token consists of a body and a signature on the body's
domain-tagged digest. Forging the signature without $\sk$ contradicts
the unforgeability of $\Sigma$ under chosen-message attack: any
adversary that forges a token can be used to construct an adversary
against $\Sigma$ by treating the domain-tagged digest as the message and
extracting the signature.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Parameter binding]
\label{thm:parameter-binding}
A valid invocation token for parameters $P$ cannot be repurposed as
a valid invocation token for parameters $P' \neq P$, except with
negligible probability.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The signed message $m_N$ contains $\Canon(\text{InvocationBody})$,
which includes $\Canon(P)$. Under collision resistance of SHA-256 and
deterministic encoding, $\Canon(P) \neq \Canon(P')$ implies $m_N \neq
m_N'$. The signature $\sigma_N$ valid on $m_N$ is invalid on $m_N'$
by the unforgeability of $\Sigma$. The redundant \texttt{params\_hash} field
provides defense-in-depth against canonical encoding bugs but is not
required for the theorem.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Chain integrity]
\label{thm:chain-integrity}
The signatures over a delegation chain cover the entire chain content:
any modification, reordering, truncation, insertion, or extension of the
chain causes \textsc{VerifyChain} to reject, except with negligible
probability.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\emph{Per-hop case.} Modifying any body changes its $m_k$ and, because
each digest commits to its predecessor, every later digest. Each
$\sigma_j$ signs $m_j$, so a verifier recomputing the digests from the
received bodies sees at least one $m_j$ that its holder never signed,
and accepting it contradicts EUF-CMA. The same argument covers
reordering, truncation and insertion: each changes the sequence of
digests the verifier recomputes, so some recomputed $m_j$ was never
signed under the key that $\mathrm{spk}(B_j)$ names. Extension requires
a new signature for the added hop, which requires that hop's secret key.

\emph{Aggregate variant.} Modification of any body changes its
canonical encoding, changing the corresponding $m_k$ (since $m_k$
hashes the body). Reordering changes the sequence of $m_k$ values (since
$m_k$ commits to $m_{k-1}$). Truncation is caught by the same
recursion. Suppose the adversary drops
$B_k$ for some interior $0 < k < N$. The verifier recomputes the
remaining messages from the bodies it actually receives, so where
$A_{k+1}$ signed
$m_{k+1} = H(\text{TAG\_DEL} \,\|\, m_k \,\|\, \Canon(B_{k+1}))$ the
verifier computes
$m'_{k+1} = H(\text{TAG\_DEL} \,\|\, m_{k-1} \,\|\, \Canon(B_{k+1}))$.
Since $m_k \neq m_{k-1}$ except with negligible probability under
collision resistance, $m'_{k+1} \neq m_{k+1}$, and every subsequent
message differs likewise. The adversary would need signatures under the
surviving hops' keys on messages those hops never signed, which
contradicts aggregate security. Dropping the first or last hop is
caught earlier still, by the position-determined tag check: a chain
whose leading token is not a session token, or whose trailing token is
not an invocation token, is rejected in phase 1 of \cref{alg:verify}.
No assumption about the secrecy of individual $\sigma_k$ values is
required, and we make none --- the accumulation scheme of
\cref{sec:aggregate-variant} would not support one. Extension requires
producing a new $\sigma$ for the extension hop; under aggregate
security, this requires the secret key of the extending party.

\emph{Both.} The position-determined tag check
(\cref{sec:protocol-digest}) rejects chains where an invocation token is
repurposed as a delegation token (or vice versa) for chain extension.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Scope monotonicity]
\label{thm:scope-monotonicity}
In any chain accepted by the verifier, every link of the containment
chain \cref{eq:chain-containment} holds in the sense of
\cref{def:subset}, where $S$ is a policy the verifier has pinned for the
issuing organization. In particular, the session scope is contained in
$S$, and the scope at hop $k+1$ is contained in the scope at hop $k$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Phase 6 of \cref{alg:verify2} rejects any session whose policy hash is
not pinned for the issuing organization. That organization is
$\mathrm{org}(\mathrm{sid}(B_0))$, read from the signed session body.
Phase 5 of \cref{alg:verify} has already required the issuer's
certificate to verify under the root the verifier holds for that
organization, and to name it as its registry. So the pins consulted are
those of the organization that actually issued the session, and a
registry trusted only for some other organization cannot redirect the
lookup. Phase 5 has also required that certificate to be of kind
\texttt{issuer}. So the session scope is one the organization's issuance
service granted, not one an agent or approver granted itself. Phase 6 then resolves $S$ by content
address, so by collision resistance of SHA-256 $S$ is the document the
pinned hash names. Finally it checks each link of
\cref{eq:chain-containment} explicitly, using the containment procedure
of \cref{sec:policy-subset}. Acceptance of the chain implies that every
one of these checks returned true, and by the soundness half of
\cref{prop:decidability} that implies containment in the sense of
\cref{def:subset}. The converse does not hold: by
\cref{rem:incompleteness} the verifier will also reject some chains
whose scopes are genuinely nested, so the guarantee is that accepted
chains are monotone, not that all monotone chains are accepted.
The integrity of the body content from which the policy hash and the
scopes are read is guaranteed by \cref{thm:chain-integrity}. The
first link is only as strong as the verifier's pinned set, which is an
assumption (\cref{sec:security-assumptions}) and not something the
protocol establishes.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Replay resistance]
\label{thm:replay}
Consider a \emph{single logical verifier} maintaining a correctly-sized
nonce cache with an atomic insert-if-absent operation --- one cache
shared across all instances that accept chains, rather than an
independent cache per replica --- that accepts a chain only when
inserting its nonce-and-invoker-key pair succeeds. It accepts at most
one chain for each such pair within the validity window, however
requests are interleaved, and rejects every chain presented after the
validity window has expired. Moreover, a chain addressed to one verifier
is rejected by every verifier with a different identifier.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Acceptance requires a successful insertion of the chain's
$(\pk_N, \text{nonce})$ pair. The insertion is atomic, so of any number
of chains carrying the same pair, whether presented one after another or
concurrently, at most one insertion succeeds while the pair remains in
the cache; the phase-3 lookup only rejects some replays earlier. The
cache TTL is the validity window plus a tolerance at least as large as
the clock disagreement among the instances sharing the cache, so the
pair is not evicted while a replay could still pass the temporal checks
at any of them, and
outside the window the verifier rejects on the expiry check before any
signature verification. For the second part, the invocation body names
its verifier in \texttt{aud}, which is part of $m_N$. Phase 1 rejects a
chain whose \texttt{aud} is not the verifier's own identifier, and
altering \texttt{aud} changes $m_N$ and invalidates $\sigma_N$ (or the
aggregate)
(\cref{thm:chain-integrity}). Distinct verifiers therefore need no
shared cache, provided they have distinct identifiers.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Approval binding]
\label{thm:approval-binding}
An approval receipt for invocation $I$ cannot be reused for any
invocation $I' \neq I$, and an invocation requiring approval from
service $s$ cannot be accepted with approval from service $s' \neq s$,
except with negligible probability.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The approval body signed by the approval service includes
the invocation digest of \cref{sec:protocol-approvals}, a tagged hash of
the invocation body without its receipts. Modification of the invocation changes
this digest, invalidating the receipt under the unforgeability of
$\Sigma$. Phase 7
checks each receipt under the key the registry currently certifies for
the service it names, with kind \texttt{approver}, so a receipt signed
by any other key, or under a revoked certificate, is rejected. The
required approval service identifiers are named by the policy $S$. The
session names $S$ by hash, but the verifier accepts the hash only if it
has pinned it for the issuing organization. $S$ is therefore a policy
the receiving organization agreed to, not one the issuer was free to
choose. Resolution is content-addressed (\cref{sec:identity-resolution}),
so no other document can be substituted behind the hash.

The requirement then propagates inward. By
\cref{thm:scope-monotonicity}, every link of the containment chain
\cref{eq:chain-containment} holds, and by the approval clause of
\cref{def:subset} each containment can only strengthen the approval
requirement. The requirement the verifier
obtains by evaluating the invocation against $\mathrm{scope}(B_{N-1})$
is therefore no weaker than the one $S$ imposes, even though the
intermediate scopes were written by the agents themselves. Routing the
approval elsewhere disturbs none of this: the policy hash is fixed at
session start and does not vary with the agent's routing choice, so a
misrouted chain arrives carrying the correct hash and the wrong receipt,
and phase 7 of \cref{alg:verify2} rejects it because no receipt signed
by a required service is present.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Threats Addressed}
\label{sec:security-threats}

The five prevented threat families of \cref{sec:threat-within-scope} are
addressed by the above theorems and the identity infrastructure of
\cref{sec:identity}, as follows. Token forgery (\threatref{1a}) is
addressed by \cref{thm:unforgeability}, and chain forgery
(\threatref{1b}) by \cref{thm:chain-integrity}. Parameter substitution
(\threatref{2a}) is addressed by \cref{thm:parameter-binding}, and scope
escalation (\threatref{2b}) by Theorems \ref{thm:chain-integrity} and
\ref{thm:scope-monotonicity} together. Cross-session replay
(\threatref{3a}) and intra-window replay (\threatref{3b}) are addressed
by \cref{thm:replay}, under the single-logical-verifier hypothesis
stated there; cross-context token reuse (\threatref{3c}) is addressed by
domain separation tags (\cref{sec:protocol-digest}); and cross-verifier
reuse (\threatref{3d}) is addressed by the audience check of phase 1,
the second part of \cref{thm:replay}. The mandatory audience clause of
every rule (\cref{sec:policy-eval}) goes further, and lets a policy
state which verifiers it authorizes at all. The prevented part
of the approval family (\threatref{4b}--\threatref{4d}) is addressed by
\cref{thm:approval-binding} together with the policy containment chain
of \cref{eq:chain-containment}; \threatref{4a} is bounded rather than
prevented and is discussed below. The prevented part of the identity
family (\threatref{5a}, \threatref{5c}, \threatref{5d}) is addressed by
proof-of-possession
(\cref{sec:identity-pop}), short-lived certificates and revocation
(\cref{sec:identity-revocation}), and optional transparency log
integration. Certificate substitution (\threatref{5d}) additionally
relies on the namespace binding of \cref{sec:identity-resolution}: a
certificate must verify under the root the verifier holds for the
organization its identifier names. Role confusion (\threatref{5e}) is
addressed by the kind checks of phases 5 and 7. They accept only an
\texttt{issuer} certificate for the session body, only \texttt{agent}
certificates for later hops, and only \texttt{approver} certificates for
receipts. Pinning adds an informal bound outside the honest-root
assumption. An issuer whose keys have been compromised can still grant
sessions only within the policies the verifier has pinned for that
issuer's own organization, and because of the kind check a compromised
agent or approver key cannot act as an issuer at all. The qualifier
depends on the namespace binding. Without it, a compromise in one
organization could draw on the policies pinned for another.

The threats of \cref{sec:threat-bounded} are bounded, not prevented.
A \emph{compromised approval service} (\threatref{4a}) can issue
receipts the human approver never authorized, and no check in
\cref{alg:verify,alg:verify2} can distinguish them from honest ones. The bound comes
from the decoupling of \cref{sec:protocol-approvals}: because approval
signatures are verified separately from the chain signatures, the
compromise cannot extend to chain forgery or scope escalation, and it
reaches only those invocations that policy routes through the affected
service. A \emph{compromised registry root} (\threatref{5b}) can
certify any identity in its own organization's namespace. Its reach is
bounded by the namespace binding of \cref{sec:identity-resolution}, which
keeps it out of every other organization's identifiers, and by pinning:
the sessions it enables stay within the policies verifiers have pinned
for that organization. \emph{Within-policy prompt injection} (\threatref{6a}) --- an adversary
inducing an agent to take an action that is within its permitted policy
but contrary to the principal's intent --- is not prevented by the
protocol. The protocol's contribution is to \emph{bound the blast
radius}: the adversary can cause only invocations that policy already
permits and, where policy requires human approval, will surface the
action to an approver who has an opportunity to refuse. Tightly-scoped
policies and appropriately-required approvals reduce the blast radius;
defending against prompt injection itself remains an active research
area outside the protocol's purview. \emph{Confused-deputy delegation}
(\threatref{6b}), where an agent is induced to delegate authority to an
adversary, is similarly bounded rather than prevented: the agent cannot
delegate more authority than it itself holds
(\cref{thm:scope-monotonicity}), limiting damage to the agent's own
permitted scope.

\subsection{Limitations of the Analysis}
\label{sec:security-limitations-analysis}

We emphasize that the analysis above consists of proof sketches under
specified assumptions, not machine-checked or peer-reviewed proofs.
Several aspects warrant scrutiny in any subsequent formalization.
\Cref{thm:chain-integrity} does not depend on proof-of-possession. In
the per-hop case it rests on each signature's unforgeability; under the
aggregate variant it rests on the messages of a chain being pairwise
distinct, which the digest recursion guarantees except with negligible
probability and which phase 8 of \cref{alg:verify2} also checks
explicitly. Proof-of-possession remains a registration requirement, but
a failure to enforce it would affect attribution (\threatref{5a}), not
chain integrity. \Cref{thm:replay}
depends on operational properties of the nonce cache (correct sizing,
crash recovery, distributed consistency across verifier replicas)
that are not enforced by the protocol but must be discharged by the
verifier implementation. The containment procedure of
\cref{sec:policy-subset} is sound but not complete with respect to
\cref{def:subset} (\cref{rem:incompleteness}); its soundness has been
argued informally here and would repay mechanization, since it is the
step on which \cref{thm:scope-monotonicity} rests.
The reference implementation's security suite
(\cref{sec:eval-correctness}) exercises every threat row against the
algorithm line that should reject it. That is evidence that the
implementation matches the specification, not a proof that the
specification is secure. Symbolic verification of the protocol in
Tamarin or ProVerif would confirm or refute these arguments and is
planned future work.

\section{Implementation and Evaluation}
\label{sec:impl}

This section describes the reference implementation
(\cref{sec:impl-implementation}) and its correctness evaluation
(\cref{sec:eval-correctness}), then a performance evaluation under an
analysis plan frozen before any measurement
(\cref{sec:eval-method,sec:eval-results,sec:eval-claims}). Exploratory
analyses, which the plan did not include, follow separately
(\cref{sec:eval-positioning,sec:eval-phases}), and the section closes
with threats to validity (\cref{sec:eval-threats}). Every table and
figure in it is generated by the benchmark harness from archived raw
data and included unedited.

\subsection{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl-implementation}

The reference implementation is a Rust workspace of nine crates:
\texttt{dc-cbor}, a strict deterministic CBOR codec that rejects every
non-canonical encoding; \texttt{dc-crypto}, the signature schemes behind
a common interface; \texttt{dc-types}, the bodies, digests, certificates
and receipts; \texttt{dc-registry}, registration with proof-of-possession,
resolution, revocation and the policy store; \texttt{dc-policy}, the
policy language of \cref{sec:policy}; \texttt{dc-chain}, the issuance,
signing and approval services; \texttt{dc-verifier}, which implements
\cref{alg:verify,alg:verify2} line by line, with one rejection variant
per rejecting line; \texttt{dc-baselines}, the comparison arms and the
prefix caches; and \texttt{dc-bench}, the benchmark harness. The verifier
is generic over the signature scheme, so every instantiation runs the
same phases 1--7 and differs only at line 49. Unsafe code is denied
throughout, except for one operating-system call in the benchmark
harness. The measurement-relevant dependencies are pinned:
\texttt{blst} 0.3.17 \citep{blst-0.3.17}, \texttt{ed25519-dalek} 2.2.0
\citep{ed25519-dalek-2.2.0} and \texttt{biscuit-auth} 6.0.0
\citep{biscuit-auth-6.0.0}, built with rustc 1.97.1.

The implementation was built specification first. Every choice the
paper left open was recorded as a numbered decision, and every defect found in the paper was logged with the
evidence for it. The review that preceded implementation, and the
implementation itself, surfaced 16 specification defects, among them a soundness gap in an earlier containment procedure
and a revocation gap, all corrected in the version presented here.

\subsection{Correctness Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval-correctness}

\paragraph{Security suite.}
Every threat of \cref{sec:threat-model}, from \threatref{1a} to
\threatref{6b}, has at least one test, as do the closed-world rule, the
structural checks, key-chain consistency and resolution, and phase
ordering. Each test asserts the exact algorithm line that rejects the
attack, or acceptance where the threat is only bounded. All 52 tests
passed when the suite's table was generated; \cref{app:security-suite}
lists them.

\paragraph{Concurrent replay.}
\Cref{thm:replay} was tested with 64 threads presenting the same chain
in each of 1,000 rounds, 64,000 verifications in all. Every round
accepted exactly one copy. Of the rejected copies, 49,638 were stopped by
the phase-3 lookup (line 17) and 13,362 only by the atomic insertion of
line 50: without atomicity, each of those would have been accepted.

\paragraph{Cache equivalence.}
Caches must change the cost of a verification, never its outcome
(\cref{sec:protocol-verification}). For each family of instantiations, a
randomized run of 10,000 chains compared the uncached verifier with the
warm one and, where they exist, with the prefix-cached one: the default
instantiation (per-call, batch and prefix-cached), the aggregate variant
(with and without its pairing and prefix caches), and individual BLS
signatures. The runs interleave clock advances, pin changes, rotations,
same-key renewals (including future-dated and shortening ones),
revocations of the newer and of the older certificate of a renewed
binding, refused re-certifications, and replays. No run found a
disagreement in decision or in rejecting line. For the aggregate
variant, a separate run of 10,000 randomized chains found that the
pairing cache agrees with direct aggregate verification on every one.

\paragraph{Differential oracle for containment.}
\Cref{tab:oracle} compares the containment procedure of
\cref{sec:policy-subset} with an enumerating oracle on 150,000 generated
scope pairs, of which 108,370 are well-formed; the validator rejected
49,467 malformed scopes. The procedure never answered true where the
oracle answered false: there were no soundness violations, and no
reflexivity failures in 250,533 checks. Its completeness is 0.9197
overall and 0.9991 when no child rule is unsatisfiable, a source of
incompleteness that \cref{rem:incompleteness} names: under our
generator, unsatisfiable child rules account for 4,138 of the 4,176
missed containments. These
rates depend on the generator: half of its pairs are independent, and in
the other half the child scope is derived from the parent by mutation,
so that containment is often true or nearly so. As evidence that the
oracle can find errors, three bugs planted in the procedure were all
caught.

\subsection{Performance Method}

\paragraph{Workloads and states.}
Four profiles: \emph{small}, one rule with two parameters and one atom;
\emph{medium}, the policy of \cref{sec:policy-example} verbatim, each
hop tightening one bound; \emph{large}, 16 rules with 4 parameters and
3 atoms each, including two approval rules, each placed before the
permissive rule it overlaps; and \emph{medium-approval}, which is medium with an
invocation that requires a finance receipt. Verifiers were measured in
four states: \emph{cold}, with empty caches; \emph{warm}, with
certificate and policy caches filled by separate warm-up chains;
\emph{warm+prefix}, in which every measured call is a prefix-cache hit;
and \emph{prefix-miss}, in which every chain has a new prefix.

\paragraph{Grid, machine and statistics.}
The grid covers $N \in \{1, 2, 3, 5, 10\}$ in each profile, with
medium-approval at $N = 3$ only: 231 latency configurations per run,
each with 1,000 warm-up and 10,000 measured verifications (fewer for the
injected-latency configurations of the cold path), over three
runs in separate processes, each in its own seeded random order. Every
timed operation is one verification call on pre-generated bytes, and
every measured verification was accepted. The machine is an Apple M4
Max (10 performance and 4 efficiency cores) under macOS 26.5.2, on AC
power in High Power mode, with release builds, fat LTO and
\texttt{target-cpu=native}. We report medians with bootstrap 95\%
confidence intervals pooled over the runs. Each ratio of the aggregating
arm to its non-aggregating counterpart is judged with a $\pm 10\%$
margin of practical significance: \emph{not a net benefit} if the
interval's lower bound exceeds 1.10, \emph{net benefit} if its upper
bound is below 0.90, and \emph{no material difference} or
\emph{inconclusive} otherwise. A verdict stands only if all three runs'
own ratios fall on the same side of the margin. The pooled intervals are
often nearly zero-width, because pooling treats the runs' samples as
independent and the timer is fine-grained, so it is the agreement of
the runs that makes the verdicts robust; the figures therefore show the
range of the three runs' medians.

\paragraph{The frozen plan and its deviations.}
The plan was frozen and tagged before any measurement. Two amendments
preceded measurement: a calibration probe, run before and after every
configuration, as a second throttling signal, and a text correction.
Two deviations followed it, both approved by the author; the first was
decided before any latency result was opened. First, the safety valve, which aborts a run
with more than 10\% of its configurations flagged as throttled, had
been implemented per process; it was changed to count per run, as the
plan specifies, after a process running the multi-threaded supplementary
arm aborted, and that process was redone in full. Second, a 200~ms busy
spin was added before every probe, because probes that followed
sleep-dominated configurations had measured an idle core's ramp-up
rather than throttling. Twenty-six configurations were flagged, every one
by the probe and none by the operating system's thermal report, and were
re-run as the plan requires; the two flagged again use their re-run
samples. After the results were reported, one report error was
corrected --- a comparison of token sizes in different units
(\cref{sec:eval-positioning}) --- without changing any measurement or verdict. Three attempts to
resume measurement were refused by the machine-state check before
anything ran.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:eval-threats}

The results come from one machine, an Apple M4 Max under macOS, with no
core pinning or frequency control; measuring threads ran at the
operating system's user-interactive quality-of-service class, and
throttling was
watched by the operating system's thermal report and by the calibration
probe. \texttt{target-cpu=native} reaches the Rust code but not
\texttt{blst}'s C and assembly, so the build flags help the Ed25519 arms
more than the BLS ones; \texttt{blst} used its armv8 assembly, and the
Ed25519 library its serial backend, so the ratios could differ on x86.
The registry is in-process, and network latency was injected as sleeps,
not measured over a network. The small and large policies and all
workloads are synthetic; only the medium profile is the paper's own
example. C-batch's batch verification has different semantics from
strict verification at the edges. The multi-threaded supplementary arm
warms the chip, so its own latencies include that thermal state; it
appears in no headline ratio. The pooled confidence intervals are
narrow because of pooling and understate between-run variation, which
the run-agreement rule guards against. Finally, arm E and AIP's
published figures measure different operations.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This section discusses related work, limitations of the protocol and
its analysis, and directions for future work.

\subsection{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

DelegationChain draws on three distinct research traditions:
capability-based authorization, pairing-based cryptographic protocols,
and agent-system security. We position our contribution relative to
each.

\paragraph{Capability-based authorization.}
The conceptual framework of attenuated capabilities, in which authority
is represented by unforgeable tokens that can be progressively
restricted as they are delegated, dates to early work on capability
operating systems and was formalized in the modern context by
\citet{miller2006robust}. The most directly relevant prior work is
Macaroons \citep{birgisson2014macaroons}, which applied attenuated
capabilities to cloud authorization using HMAC chains. A Macaroon
carries ``caveats,'' predicates that restrict its use; each delegation
appends additional caveats, and verification recomputes the HMAC chain
using a shared secret known to the verifier. Macaroons demonstrated
that attenuated delegation could be made cryptographic and efficient.
Macaroons also provide \emph{third-party caveats}, in which a caveat
names a principal other than the target service and is satisfied by a
discharge macaroon obtained from that principal
(\cref{sec:bg-capabilities}). This is the mechanism by which Macaroons
reach across trust boundaries, and any comparison that treats Macaroons
as a single-domain-only design is inaccurate. DelegationChain extends
the model in three principal directions: from shared-secret HMAC to
public-key signatures; from caveats that constrain future use to a
binding of the specific invocation's parameter values; and from informal
caveat languages to a typed declarative policy language with termination
and containment guarantees.

The substantive difference from third-party caveats is the shape of the
verification path rather than its reach. Discharging a third-party
caveat requires the holder to contact the named principal and collect a
discharge macaroon before presenting the token; the third party must be
available at that moment, and each additional third party adds a round
trip. DelegationChain moves that authority into signatures that the
verifier checks locally against registry-resolved public keys, so the
request path carries no discharge-collection step. The cost of this
choice is the identity infrastructure of \cref{sec:identity}, which
Macaroons does not require.

UCAN \citep{ucan-spec} also extends capability delegation to a
public-key setting, originally for Web3 contexts. UCAN 1.0 signs
tokens canonically encoded in DAG-CBOR, inside a Varsig-headed envelope,
with Ed25519 recommended and P-256 and secp256k1 also required, and
expresses delegation attenuation through scoped capability tokens. Our work
differs from UCAN in three respects. UCAN 1.0 does specify a policy
language over command arguments, but we did not find a defined
containment relation between capabilities, which is what
\cref{sec:policy-subset} supplies and what the scope monotonicity check
requires. We target MCP rather than Web3 transport. And we include a
federated identity infrastructure with proof-of-possession, policy
ceilings pinned by the receiving organization, and revocation by
identity--key binding. UCAN's specification recommends memoizing
validated delegation proofs, so that tokens sharing a proof need not
recheck it \citep{ucan-spec}; that is the prefix caching the default
instantiation of DelegationChain uses (\cref{sec:eval-results}).

\paragraph{Authorization certificates and delegation chains.}
The problem of composing delegation chains and computing the authority
that survives composition is older than this literature. SPKI/SDSI
\citep{rfc2693} defines authorization certificates in which the
principal is a public key rather than a name, and specifies a
\emph{5-tuple reduction} algorithm that composes a chain of delegation
certificates into a single effective authorization by intersecting the
authorization tags at each step, subject to a delegation bit controlling
whether further delegation is permitted. Reduction is the direct
ancestor of the scope-monotonicity check of \cref{sec:policy-subset},
and the correspondence is close: SPKI intersects tags where we require
containment between scope expressions. The differences are that SPKI
computes the intersection, producing the resulting authority, where our
verifier only checks a containment relation that the delegator has
already asserted; that SPKI's tag \emph{*-forms} (prefix, range, set)
are a fixed and deliberately small constraint vocabulary, where our
policy language is typed and admits conditions over named parameter
paths.

X.509 proxy certificates \citep{rfc3820} apply the same idea within Web
PKI and were deployed at scale in grid computing. An end-entity
certificate signs a short-lived proxy certificate, which may itself sign
another, producing a delegation chain that a relying party validates as
an ordinary X.509 path; attenuation is expressed through an embedded
\texttt{ProxyCertInfo} policy field. This is the closest classical prior
art to the certificate-chain-plus-policy design of \cref{sec:identity}.
Two differences are worth naming. RFC 3820 leaves the content of the
policy field opaque --- it names a policy language by OID and carries
its bytes, without specifying one --- so nothing in the standard
provides the decidable containment check our \cref{sec:policy} is built
around. And proxy certificate chains root in a CA hierarchy, where our
registry model is federated and explicitly declines a global trust
anchor (\cref{sec:identity-federated}).

Biscuit \citep{biscuit-spec} is the closest contemporary system in the
capability-token line. Biscuit tokens are public-key verifiable, support
offline attenuation --- a holder may append a block of additional checks
at any time, restricting the token without contacting the issuer, and
cannot remove a block without invalidating the signature --- and carry
an authorization language based on a purpose-built Datalog with
specified evaluation semantics. Biscuit's Datalog is more expressive
than our policy language, and its formal treatment of evaluation is more
developed than the sketch we give. The difference runs the other way on
one axis: Biscuit does not bind a token to a specific invocation's
parameter values, since attenuation constrains future use rather than
committing to one call. \Cref{sec:eval-positioning} positions a Biscuit
mapping of our chains against the default instantiation. We regard Biscuit's Datalog as the more serious policy
design and note that a containment check over it would be a
substantially harder problem than the one \cref{prop:decidability}
addresses.

ZCAP-LD \citep{zcapld}, developed in the W3C Credentials Community
Group, expresses object capabilities as linked-data documents with
invocation and delegation proofs, and shares the attenuated-delegation
model. Its current draft attenuates in three fixed ways. A delegated
capability may append a path or query suffix to its parent's invocation
target, must carry an expiry, and may allow only actions its parent
allowed. That vocabulary is small and its containment is
straightforward: a prefix check, a date comparison and a subset test.
ZCAP is therefore not outside the decidability constraints of
\cref{sec:policy}, as an earlier draft with extensible caveats would
have been. What it lacks is any way to constrain an invocation's
arguments beyond a prefix of the target URL, with no range or membership
conditions of the kind \cref{sec:policy} provides. It also remains a
community-group draft rather than a standard.

\paragraph{Proof-carrying authorization.}
A separate tradition places the burden of justifying access on the
requester. \citet{appel1999pca} introduced proof-carrying
authentication, in which a request arrives accompanied by a proof, in a
higher-order logic, that it is authorized; the verifier need only check
the proof, and the framework is expressive enough to encode Taos, SPKI,
SDSI and X.509. \citet{bauer2003pca} developed the approach into a
deployed system for web access control. The comparison is instructive
because the trust structure is the same as ours --- the verifier checks
locally, without consulting the issuer --- while the mechanism is the
opposite: proof-carrying authorization gains expressiveness by admitting
an arbitrary logic and pushing proof search to the requester, where we
give up expressiveness (\cref{sec:policy-limitations}) to keep the
verifier's work bounded by a syntactic check. A DelegationChain chain
can be read as a very restricted proof, in which the only inference
rules are signature verification and scope containment.

\paragraph{Cryptographic infrastructure.}
The BLS signature scheme is due to \citet{boneh2001bls}, and aggregation
over distinct messages, with its security proof, to
\citet{bgls2003aggregate}; that proof is the one the aggregate
variant's chain integrity argument uses. Rogue-key defenses for the same-message setting ---
proof-of-possession \citep{ristenpart2007pop} and the alternative of
\citet{boneh2018compact} --- are not needed for it, and we use
proof-of-possession only for attribution (\cref{sec:identity-pop}). We use the signing variant standardized by the
CFRG \citep{ietf-bls-signature}, the BLS12-381 curve parameters of
\citet{ietf-pairing-curves}, and the hash-to-curve construction of
RFC 9380 \citep{rfc9380}.

The problem our chain digest solves in the aggregate variant ---
producing a single compact signature over an \emph{ordered} sequence of
signers on distinct
messages, such that the order is itself authenticated --- is the problem
that \emph{sequential aggregate signatures} were introduced to solve.
\citet{lmrs2004seqagg} gave the first construction, in which each signer
folds its signature into the aggregate it receives from its predecessor,
using certified trapdoor permutations; the order of signing is bound by
the construction itself. \citet{boldyreva2007ordered} extended the line
to ordered multisignatures and identity-based sequential aggregates in
the pairing setting. We should therefore be precise about what is and is
not new here. Ordered, aggregated signature chains are not new, and we
do not claim them.

What we do claim is narrower: a specification of how, in the aggregate
variant, to obtain order binding for delegation chains using a recursive
hash chain over
domain-separated token bodies together with \emph{plain} BLS
aggregation, rather than a dedicated sequential aggregate scheme. The
reason for the choice is practical rather than theoretical. The
construction needs no trapdoor permutations and no scheme-specific
sequential signing operation; it uses the ordinary signing and
aggregation operations of the CFRG BLS draft
\citep{ietf-bls-signature}, so it can be built on any conforming
library, and the order binding lives in data the verifier already has to
hash. What we give up is that the hash chain must be recomputed by the
verifier from the received bodies, whereas a native sequential aggregate
carries order binding inside the signature object. \Cref{sec:impl}
settles the question of cost for our setting: our construction does not
pay for itself against per-hop Ed25519 signatures, and with caching the
comparison is decided by its verification floor of one pairing check
per invocation. Any aggregate scheme whose verification needs at least
one pairing check shares that floor; schemes built on trapdoor
permutations \citep{lmrs2004seqagg} do not, and we did not measure
them. Certificate Transparency \citep{rfc9162}
provided the model for our optional transparency log integration.

\paragraph{MCP and agent-system security.}
The security gap in cross-organizational MCP deployments has been
empirically characterized by recent measurement work.
\citet{zhou2026mcpauth} surveyed 7,973 live remote MCP servers and found
40.55\% exposing tools without authentication; of the OAuth-enabled
servers they were able to probe actively, every one exhibited at least
one authentication flaw, and the disclosure process yielded nine CVE
identifiers. Single-organization runtime enforcement approaches
\citep{buhler2026agentbound} provide defense-in-depth within an
organization's perimeter but do not provide cross-organizational
cryptographic proofs and are not designed to extend across
organizational boundaries.

A second group of proposals secures the other direction of the MCP
relationship. \citet{maloyan2026breaking} give a security analysis of
the MCP specification and propose a backward-compatible extension ---
called MCPSec in their abstract and AttestMCP in the body of the paper
--- in which servers prove their claimed capabilities with certificates
from a capability authority, and JSON-RPC messages carry HMAC
authentication bound to server identity. \citet{metere2026admission}
similarly lets a host decide which tool servers, and which of their
tools, it will admit. Both protect the host from untrustworthy servers.
DelegationChain protects the server from unauthorized callers, so the
two directions are complementary.

Closer to our direction, ACLE-MCP \citep{ding2026aclemcp} addresses what
its authors call the post-authorization execution trust gap: OAuth
authorization does not ensure that a later call is executed by the
workload the relying party meant to trust. For each protected call it
issues a short-lived, sender-constrained capability lease binding the
expected workload, the operation, object and parameter bounds, and
downstream constraints; a provider-side gate consumes the lease before
the tool runs. The differences are of mechanism and of scope. ACLE-MCP's
lease carries parameter bounds set when the lease is issued; our
invocation token binds exact values, which the verifier then checks
against the policy's conditions. Its leases are issued per call, where
our delegation prefix is built once and only the invocation token is
signed per call, without contacting any issuer. Conversely, ACLE-MCP
attests the executing workload, which DelegationChain does not address
at all.

\citet{llambi2026cva} formalize cryptographically verifiable agent
authorization as a relation binding an agent principal, a concrete
request, an execution context and the satisfaction of a policy, with a
zero-knowledge proof of concept. Their request binding corresponds
closely to what we call parameter binding. Their separation of request
binding from runtime execution binding names the gap that ACLE-MCP
addresses and that we leave open. \citet{zhouz2026governing} proposes
governance requirements for agents that act across organizational
boundaries --- capability integrity, behavioral verifiability and
interaction auditability. Among its results is that one unverifiable
interior agent breaks end-to-end verification for every downstream node,
the multi-agent counterpart of our requirement that every hop of a chain
verify. Binding authorization to an invocation's parameter values is not
among its requirements.

The closest contemporary work is the Agent Identity Protocol of
\citet{prakash2026aip}, which proposes invocation-bound capability
tokens for delegation across MCP and A2A. AIP has two wire formats. Its
compact mode, for single-hop cases, is an EdDSA-signed JWT carrying
issuer, subject, scope, a budget, a maximum delegation depth and
timestamps. Its chained mode is a Biscuit token with Datalog policies,
in which each delegating agent appends a signed block that attenuates
scope. Verification is local, with no authorization-server round trip.
Identifiers either resolve over HTTPS (\texttt{aip:web}) or are the
public key itself (\texttt{aip:key}), which is the same shape as our
design. The authors list identity resolution, protocol bindings,
completion blocks recording provenance, policy profiles and the compact
mode as what AIP adds to Biscuit, and they ship interoperable Rust and
Python implementations.

Three differences matter. First, despite the name, neither AIP format
signs the parameter values of an invocation. Scopes name capabilities
such as a tool, and --- as with Biscuit, of which the chained mode is an
instance --- attenuation constrains future use rather than committing to
a particular call. Parameter binding (\cref{sec:protocol-params}) is the
property on which the two designs differ most. Second, DelegationChain's
policy language has a formally specified containment check
(\cref{sec:policy-subset}); AIP's Datalog policies, inherited from
Biscuit, specify evaluation, and we did not find a containment check
between them. Third, AIP has no registration step with
proof-of-possession and no federation model, and it defers revocation
to a later version, relying for now on short-lived tokens;
\cref{sec:identity} supplies registration with proof-of-possession,
pinned policy ceilings, and revocation by identity--key binding. Both
designs now sign each hop individually and have implementations;
\cref{sec:eval-positioning} positions them, with the caveat that the
published figures and ours time different operations.

\paragraph{Workload and identity infrastructure.}
SPIFFE/SPIRE \citep{spiffe} provides workload identity through
short-lived X.509 certificates and JWT-format SVIDs. While SPIFFE
addresses workload identity within a trust domain, it does not
provide parameter-bound authorization or delegation chain integrity.
OAuth 2.1 \citep{oauth21} addresses caller
identity and scope, may require synchronous token introspection on the
verification path, and does not provide parameter binding.

For crossing organizational boundaries, the OAuth working group's
identity-chaining draft \citep{oauth-identity-chaining} combines token
exchange with the JWT authorization grant. The client's authorization
server in one domain issues a signed grant, which the authorization
server in the next domain validates and exchanges for an access token,
and the pattern repeats at each boundary. Scope can be narrowed at each
exchange, so attenuated delegation across organizations is expressible
in OAuth. The differences from DelegationChain are structural. Every
boundary costs round trips to authorization servers on both sides. What
reaches the resource is a token from its own domain's authorization
server, which vouches for the earlier hops rather than letting the
resource verify them. And nothing binds the token to one invocation's
argument values. We view
DelegationChain not as a replacement for these systems but as
complementary infrastructure for the specific use case of verifiable
agent-to-tool invocation across organizations.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

We discuss limitations of the work along several dimensions.

\paragraph{Offline verification is qualified.}
We claim verification that requires no synchronous call to the issuing
authority \emph{on the dominant path}, and the qualification is load
bearing. Two dependencies remain, and a third can be avoided.
Certificate resolution (\cref{sec:identity-resolution}) queries the
issuing registry on a cache miss. Revocation
(\cref{sec:identity-revocation}) requires polling, push, or log
monitoring, which is a continuous background dependency rather than a
per-request one. Policy resolution is the avoidable one. Pinned
policies can be fetched when they are pinned
(\cref{sec:identity-resolution}), and only a verifier that defers the
fetch to first use pays a round trip, on that call. In the steady state --- a partner whose
certificates and policies are already cached --- verification touches
none of these and is genuinely local. On a cold cache it is not, and
statements elsewhere in this paper about offline verification should be
read with that scope. A cold verifier also verifies $N + 1$
certificates before the policy phase (\cref{sec:protocol-verification}).

The obvious repair is to carry certificates in the chain, as X.509 does,
so that a verifier needs the registry root key but not the registry.
This would make verification offline modulo revocation, at a cost in
bytes: in the reference implementation's encoding a certificate is 185
bytes under Ed25519 (249 under the aggregate variant's BLS keys), and a
chain would carry $N + 1$ of them. We note it as a deployment option
rather than adopting it.

\paragraph{Security arguments are sketches, not proofs.}
\Cref{sec:security} presents proof sketches that reduce protocol
security to standard cryptographic assumptions. These sketches have
not been mechanized in a proof assistant nor have they been verified
by independent cryptographer review. Symbolic protocol verification
using Tamarin or ProVerif is planned and is expected to reveal at
minimum the kind of subtle interaction issues that hand-analysis
typically misses (e.g., key-compromise impersonation paths, unknown
key-share variants). We treat the current analysis as a starting
point for formal verification, not as a substitute for it.

\paragraph{Policy language expressiveness.}
The policy language of \cref{sec:policy} is deliberately bounded. It
cannot express semantic conditions requiring evaluation by an LLM or
external system (``is this email professional?''), stateful
conditions referring to data outside the invocation (``is this user
under their monthly limit?''), or conditions requiring synchronous
queries to external systems. These limitations are intentional: each
relaxation would compromise the decidability, termination, or
side-effect-freedom guarantees on which the security analysis depends.
Where richer policy expressions are required in practice, we
anticipate them being enforced at the tool implementation layer
\emph{after} the protocol's stateless check has succeeded. A
fully-integrated treatment of stateful policy may require extensions
to the protocol --- including verifiable LLM attestation for
semantic conditions --- which we leave as future work.

\paragraph{Threat model boundaries.}
Several classes of threats are explicitly out of scope
(\cref{sec:threat-outside}), including fully-compromised HSMs,
compromised signing service infrastructure, side-channel attacks,
and attacks on human approvers' devices. The protocol provides no
defense against these and relies on operational measures (HSM
hardening, key custody discipline, secure approver workflows) for
mitigation. Within-policy prompt injection is bounded but not
prevented (\cref{sec:security-threats}); the protocol's contribution
is to ensure that prompt-injected agents cannot exceed their permitted
policy, not to prevent prompt injection itself.

\paragraph{Post-quantum security.}
Neither Ed25519 nor BLS12-381 is post-quantum secure. Per-hop signing
lets the default instantiation adopt a post-quantum signature scheme
such as ML-DSA \citep{fips204} without changing the protocol, at a cost
in bytes per hop. The aggregate variant has no standardized post-quantum
counterpart.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
The evaluation's own limitations --- one machine, an in-process
registry, synthetic workloads --- are set out in
\cref{sec:eval-threats}.

\paragraph{Bootstrapping and adoption.}
The federated registry model requires both communicating organizations
to operate compatible registries (or to participate in a shared
transparency log). Adoption of the protocol in cross-organizational
agent ecosystems thus faces a bootstrapping problem: early adopters
have limited peers to communicate with, and the value of adoption
grows with peer density. We do not address bootstrapping strategies
in this paper, but observe that the same dynamic affects all
cross-organizational identity protocols, including SPIFFE and
SAML federation. Successful adoption is likely to require either
integration into a widely-deployed platform (e.g., as a feature of
an MCP-compliant agent framework) or coordination through standards
bodies.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

We identify several directions for future work, in approximate order
of priority.

\emph{Symbolic verification.} Modeling the protocol in Tamarin or
ProVerif and proving the security properties of \cref{sec:security}
will validate (or, more usefully, falsify) the hand-written proof
sketches, and can now also check the algorithm as implemented against
the model.

\emph{Wider evaluation.} The measurements of \cref{sec:impl} come from
one machine. Repeating them on Linux and x86, and in a networked
deployment with a real registry, would test how far the verdicts carry.

\emph{Policy checks before identity resolution.} The policy checks of
lines 30--37 need no certificates. Running them before identity
resolution would spare a cold verifier the certificate checks on
policy-violating chains (\cref{sec:protocol-verification}), at the cost
of renumbering the algorithm.

\emph{Containment completeness.} Skipping child rules with
unsatisfiable clauses in step 3(a) of the containment procedure would be
sound, and would remove one of the sources of incompleteness that
\cref{rem:incompleteness} names.

\emph{A post-quantum instantiation.} Instantiating $\Sigma$ with a
post-quantum scheme such as ML-DSA \citep{fips204}, and measuring its
cost in bytes and time per hop.

\emph{Differential privacy on audit trails.} The protocol generates
detailed audit records of every accepted invocation. For deployments
in regulated industries, the ability to produce compliance attestations
(``$X\%$ of invocations were authorized'') without exposing the
content of individual invocations would have practical value.
Standard differential privacy mechanisms apply; their integration into
the protocol's audit format is straightforward but was deferred from
this work for scope reasons.

\emph{Verifiable LLM attestation for semantic policy conditions.}
The most significant restriction of the policy language is its
inability to express semantic conditions. Recent work on verifiable
LLM inference (zkML and related approaches) may enable a future
extension in which semantic policy conditions are evaluated by an LLM
whose evaluation is itself cryptographically attested. This is
speculative; the practicality of zkML at the latencies required for
authorization protocols is not currently established.

\emph{Cross-registry naming and discovery.} This paper does not
address how organizations choose and namespace their
\texttt{organization-id} values. In practice deployments will use
DNS-style hierarchical naming or other conventions, but standardizing
this is needed before adoption at scale.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Cross-organizational agent invocation needs authorization that a
receiving organization can verify on its own: bound to the exact
parameters of the call, constrained by policies both sides can reason
about, and resistant to tampering with the chain of delegation behind
it. DelegationChain provides this with digest-chained, per-hop signed
delegation chains; a typed policy language whose containment check is
polynomial and sound, with closed-world parameters and mandatory
audience clauses; and a federated registry with proof-of-possession,
pinned policy ceilings and revocation by identity--key binding.

We implemented the protocol, tested it against every threat in its model
and against a differential oracle for its policy language, and measured
it under a plan fixed before measurement. The measurement overturned the
design choice the protocol was first built around: aggregating BLS
signatures across the chain is not a net benefit in any configuration we
measured, because a pairing-based verification floor costs far more
than the bytes it saves. We report that result in full and specify
DelegationChain over per-hop Ed25519 signatures instead.

The analysis remains a set of proof sketches, and the measurements come
from one machine. Symbolic verification and a networked evaluation are
the next steps. The design space for inter-agent authorization is still
being mapped, and DelegationChain is one point in it, now a measured
one.
